Lab 10 · Spectra

A bench spectroscope, five gas tubes, and two kinds of lamp. Plus the reason every astronomer loves diffraction gratings: light carries the fingerprint of whatever made it.

Words in this lab

  • spectrum — light spread out by color, red through violet.
  • wavelength — the distance between two wave peaks. Color tells you wavelength.
  • element — a pure substance that cannot be broken down, like hydrogen or helium.
  • continuous spectrum — an unbroken rainbow from red to violet.
  • emission line spectrum — bright colored lines on a dark field, made by a hot, thin gas.
  • absorption line spectrum — dark lines cut out of a rainbow by a cooler gas.
  • diffraction grating — a slide with thousands of fine grooves that spreads light into a spectrum.

What this lab is about

Hold a diffraction-grating spectroscope up to a row of light sources. You will see an incandescent bulb, tubes of glowing hydrogen, helium, neon, mercury, and krypton, and a fluorescent lamp. Each one paints a different picture across the viewport. Your job is to describe the pictures. Record where the bright lines sit. Then use those line patterns as fingerprints. Match unknown spectra to elements, and say what lines in a star's spectrum would tell an astronomer.

The physics: three kinds of spectra

Continuous. A hot, dense object glows at every visible wavelength at once. Think of a bulb filament, or a star's visible surface. The rainbow runs unbroken from red to violet. Its balance of colors tracks temperature. A hotter glow looks bluer.

Emission lines. A hot, thin gas is different. Its atoms can only hold certain electron energies. So the light they emit comes only at certain wavelengths. You see a few colored hairlines on a black field. The set of wavelengths is fixed by the element's electron structure. So every element gets a unique pattern, as individual as a fingerprint.

Absorption lines. Run a continuous glow through a cooler gas. The gas removes exactly the wavelengths it would itself emit. It punches dark lines into the rainbow. Same fingerprint, flipped to negative. (This lab views continuous and emission spectra. The star question previews absorption.)

How the viewer works. The grating is a slide ruled with thousands of fine grooves. Light waves leaving neighboring grooves interfere. The direction where they add up depends on wavelength, so the colors leave at different angles. One consequence trips people up: a grating bends red the most. A glass prism does the opposite. It bends violet the most. If a write-up claims violet was "bent most" while using a grating viewer, the author was thinking of a prism.

What you will actually see

Hydrogen shows just four visible lines: one red, one blue-green, and two violet (the Balmer series). Helium adds more lines, with a prominent yellow one. Neon blankets the orange-red. That is neon-sign color. Mercury has a fierce blue-green line and a yellow pair. Krypton pairs a strong violet-blue duo with an orange cluster. The fluorescent lamp is the mixed case. Its white phosphor coating glows in broad bands, while mercury vapor inside contributes sharp lines. It is continuous and emission at once.

Why this matters to astronomers: spectra are the one place where astronomy can touch matter it will never visit. Line patterns say which elements are in a star or cloud. The exact pattern says it is hydrogen, not something that merely looks reddish. And when the whole pattern shifts toward red, it measures how fast the source moves away. That is exactly how astronomers discovered the expansion of the universe (Lab 15). (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 5-6, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Sketching a blend of all the colors for a gas tube. Gases show separated lines, not paint.
  • Recording the color but not a wavelength position. The grating has a scale - use it.
  • Confusing the grating's bend order (red most) with the prism's (violet most).
  • Calling the fluorescent lamp "continuous" and missing mercury's green line sitting on top.
  • Matching an unknown by overall tint instead of the exact set of line positions.
  • Assuming unseen lines are absent. Ultraviolet and infrared lines are real and measurable.
