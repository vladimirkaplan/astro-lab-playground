Lab 15 · Hubble's Law

Dots on a balloon become galaxies in an expanding universe. Measure, plot, and fit a slope. Then read the age of the balloon cosmos off that slope.

Words in this lab

  • expansion — space itself stretching and carrying galaxies apart.
  • recession speed — how fast a galaxy's distance from us grows.
  • redshift — light stretched to longer, redder waves by expansion. It reports speed away from us.
  • megaparsec — a distance unit: one million parsecs, about 3.26 million light-years.
  • Hubble constant (H) — the slope that links galaxy speed and distance.
  • slope — rise over run: how much one value grows per step of the other.
  • spectrum — light spread out by color, showing dark lines.

What this lab is about

Draw ten spots on a balloon. One is home: the Milky Way. The rest are galaxies. Measure the string distance from home to every galaxy. Inflate the balloon and measure again. Divide the change in distance by the seconds inflation took. Now every galaxy has a distance and a recession speed. Recession speed means how fast its distance from home grows. Plot the pairs and the points line up. Farther galaxies recede faster, in proportion. That proportionality is Hubble's law. Its slope is the Hubble constant.

The physics: velocity = H times distance

Space itself stretches. Everything inside it that is not bound together drifts apart. The rate depends on how much new space appears in between. Double the distance and you double the stretching in between. So speed scales with distance: V = H · D. One consequence students miss: no place is special. An observer on any balloon dot sees the same law. Uniform expansion looks the same from every point. There is no center of the surface. The surface has no edge either.

Inflate from radius factor 1 to factor a over time t. Measure the velocities against the final distances. Then the arithmetic works out to H = (a - 1) / (a · t). The lab then treats 1/H as the age of the balloon universe. Ask how far back you must unwind the expansion before all distances shrink to zero. That many years is the age.

On the real sky, Hubble's constant sits near 70 km/s per megaparsec. The conversion to years is one neat line: 1/H ≈ 977.8 / H0 gigayears. So H = 70 gives roughly 14 billion years. That is within a rounding error of the measured 13.8. Modern teams disagree at the 5-to-10-percent level. Early-universe and late-universe methods give different numbers. This is the Hubble tension. It is exactly the kind of live disagreement that makes a constant famous.

Why this matters to astronomers: velocity can act as a distance tool. Use v = H·D and you can reach out to any faint galaxy with a spectrum. Measure the redshift first. Expansion stretches a galaxy's light into longer, redder waves, and the stretch reports the speed. Lab 9 gave you the lines to measure it on. Then divide by H, done. And 1/H gives the age of the universe with one assumption: that expansion ran at a steady pace. That is close enough to be meaningful, and wrong enough to have launched a Nobel Prize. The teams that found the expansion accelerating won it. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 22, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Letting the best-fit line find its own intercept. Expansion says zero distance means zero speed, so pin the line through the origin.
  • Measuring the chord through the balloon instead of the string along the skin. The analogy lives on the surface.
  • Plotting velocity against the first distances. The law pairs v with the distances measured with that velocity.
  • Reporting the slope as an age without flipping it. Age is 1 over the slope, not the slope.
  • Carrying balloon units (cm/s) into cosmic units (km/s/Mpc) without converting anything.
  • Thinking the dots slide across the surface. On the real sky, unbound galaxies are carried apart by growth in space itself.
