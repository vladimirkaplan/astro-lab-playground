Lab 14 · Galaxy identification

Look at photos of ten real galaxies. Learn the galaxy shapes. Then sort each picture by family and stage, and name each galaxy - from pictures, not notes.

Words in this lab

  • galaxy — a huge island of stars, gas, and dust held together by gravity.
  • elliptical (E) — a smooth, round-to-oval glow of old stars. No arms.
  • spiral (S) — a flat disc with a round bulge and winding arms.
  • barred spiral (SB) — a spiral with a straight lane of light across the middle.
  • bulge — the round, bright center of a spiral galaxy.
  • dust lane — a dark band of dust that blocks starlight.
  • irregular — a galaxy with no orderly shape.

What this lab is about

Hubble sorted galaxies by how they look. His chart is called the tuning fork, and we still use it. The handle holds the ellipticals, E0 to E7. An elliptical is a smooth glow of old stars, round to oval. It has no arms and barely any gas. The two prongs hold the spirals. Ordinary spirals (S) sit on one prong, and barred spirals (SB) sit on the other. Each prong has three stages. An a spiral has a big bright bulge, and its arms are wound too tight to count. A c spiral is mostly arms. Its arms are loose and patchy. Gas and newborn stars fill them, and its bulge is small. Stage b sits in between. S0 (or SA0/SB0) sits at the fork point: a disc with a bulge but no arms at all. Off to the side sit the irregulars and peculiars. These have no orderly shape to grade. Many look messy because a companion galaxy recently shook them.

Reading an edge-on disc

A spiral seen face-on is obvious. You see the disc and arms spread open. Seen edge-on, from its side, it turns into a lens. The middle is bright and long: that is the bulge, seen from its side. The ends taper to thin points. Very often a dark straight dust lane cuts across the light. That line is the dust of the whole disc, pressed into one row across the middle. Lens shape plus dust lane means “disc, seen sideways”. It does not mean “blob”. Two galaxies in this set are edge-on: M104 and NGC 253. Both are ordinary spirals. Their strange look is only their angle. If you tipped either one flat toward you, you would count arms on it.

Why M87 is not “just blurry”

The first time students see a giant elliptical, they say “out of focus”. But blur is an excuse about the tool. Ellipticals survive sharp focus. Even in the sharpest image, M87 shows no cold gas and no dust lane. It has no spiral arms and no star-making knots. What remains is a smooth glow and its old red stars. That emptiness is the type. A blurry spiral still shows dust and hints of arms once you stretch the image. An elliptical never does. M87 does have one famous non-smooth feature: a jet from its central black hole. But a jet is an active core, not a spiral arm. Type lives in the pattern of structure, or in the honest lack of it. Color or sharpness alone decides nothing. Two galaxies can share a color and sit at opposite ends of the fork.

How astronomers actually classify

Shape comes first. The study of shape is called morphology: symmetry, how much of the light the bulge holds, bars, lanes, arms. That is what you do on the Simulate tab. The stage letters are judgement calls with fuzzy borders. A b/c object is graded honestly: you pick b or c, and you defend it. Peculiar galaxies get tick marks and footnotes, not gentle lies. Color and spectra come after, as cross-checks. Blue means gas and young stars, so usually late-type or irregular. Red and dead usually means early-type.

Why this matters to astronomers: the tuning fork is not a family tree. Ellipticals did not grow into spirals. Still, every distance ladder, every survey, and every galaxy-evolution story files its objects by this shape language first. Reading a galaxy type off a picture is a very old measuring skill. It is the oldest one for galaxies outside the Milky Way. It starts exactly where this lab starts. Decide: disc or not. Bar or not. Tight or loose. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Calling an edge-on spiral “peculiar” because it looks odd. Odd is often just orientation; find the dust lane first.
  • Deciding elliptical-vs-spiral from colour alone. A smooth red blob and a smooth red elliptical are not the same call. Look for disc, dust, and arms.
  • Missing the bar because you looked for arms first. The bar is a straight lane of light through the core, and arms spring off its ends.
  • Reading a/b/c backwards: a = tight arms, big bulge; c = loose arms, small bulge.
  • Sorting M82 as “S, because it is elongated”. Elongation alone is not a disc. Ragged gas lanes with no pattern mean peculiar.
  • Forgetting that S0 exists, then forcing an armless disc into “a”.
