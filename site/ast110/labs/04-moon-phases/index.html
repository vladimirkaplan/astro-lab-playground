Lab 4 · Phases of the Moon

The Moon keeps a schedule. Shape, rise time, and daylight appearances all follow from one angle between it, you, and the Sun.

Words in this lab

  • phase — the lit shape of the Moon you see on a given night.
  • elongation — how far the Moon sits from the Sun in your sky, in degrees.
  • waxing — growing brighter, heading for full.
  • waning — shrinking back toward new.
  • orbit — the path one object follows as it goes around another.
  • meridian — the north-south line that runs over your head.
  • transit — the moment something crosses your meridian.

What this lab is about

Half of the Moon is always lit. It is a ball in sunlight. The phase is how much of that lit half your seat on Earth can see. One number controls it: the elongation, the Sun-to-Moon angle measured at Earth. That single angle decides the shape, the rise time, and the set time. It also decides whether you can catch the Moon in the daytime.

The schedule that falls out

  • New Moon, elongation 0: the lit side faces away from you. It rises around 7 AM and sets around 7 PM, lost in the day sky.
  • First quarter, 90: the right half is lit. It rises around noon, owns the early evening sky, and sets around midnight.
  • Full, 180: opposite the Sun. It rises at sunset, is highest at midnight, and sets at sunrise. It is up all night.
  • Last quarter, 270: the left half is lit. It rises around midnight and sets around noon - on duty for dawn people.

Everything in between fills in smoothly. The transit happens about elongation/15 hours after noon. The transit is the crossing of your meridian, the line overhead. Rise and set sit six hours on either side of it. Moonrise also drifts about 50 minutes later each day. Earth spins, but the Moon slides ~12 degrees eastward along its orbit at the same time. The Moon must "catch up" that distance before it returns to your meridian.

Two rules prevent most failed observations. First, the lit side always points toward the Sun. Second, the phase tells you the hour. A thin crescent low in the west an hour after sunset is a young waxing crescent leaving for the day. The same shape low in the east before dawn is the waning crescent clocking in.

Why this matters to astronomers: the lunar cycle is the original calendar. The same "who is where relative to whom" geometry also predicts eclipses - the reasoning that turned sky-watching into science. Tides also run on this clock. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 1 and Ch. 4, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Blaming Earth's shadow for phases. That is an eclipse. The phase shadow-line is always a diameter of the disk.
  • Saying the Moon makes its own light.
  • Drawing the full Moon on the daytime side of a top-view diagram.
  • Giving a rise time your phase cannot support, like last quarter rising at 9 AM.
  • Forgetting the ~50-minute daily delay on moonrise.
  • Saying "I looked at 9 PM and saw nothing" with a waxing crescent. It set hours earlier.
