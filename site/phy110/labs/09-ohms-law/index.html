Lab 9 · Ohm’s law from a V-I line

Wire a supply, an ammeter and a voltmeter to a fixed resistor. Dial the printed set-points, log V and I at each one, and pull the resistance out of the slope of the V-I line through the origin. The 100 mA ammeter stop is the reason the small run ends at 5.5 V.

Words in this lab

  • ohm (Ω) — the unit of resistance. One ohm passes 1 A at 1 V.
  • Ohm’s law — the line V = R·I: at fixed R, voltage scales with current.
  • V-I line — the graph with volts vertical and amperes horizontal. Its slope is the resistance.
  • ammeter — the current meter, in mA or A. It wires in series, in the line of the current.
  • voltmeter — the voltage meter. It wires in parallel, across the resistor.
  • set-point — a supply voltage the manual lists for the run.
  • tap key — the switch that closes the circuit while you read the meters.

What you do at the bench

The bench has a DC supply with a 0 to 12 V knob, and a resistor bay. The bay takes slide-in 200 Ω and 100 Ω modules. An ammeter on the 100 mA scale and a voltmeter on the 15 V scale read the run. A tap key closes the circuit while you read the meters.

In Part I you wire the circuit with red and black leads: the ammeter in series with the resistor, the voltmeter across it. The auto-check button names any lead that is missing or wrong.

In Part II you slide in the 200 Ω module. Dial each printed set-point, close the tap key, read the meters, and log the point.

In Part III you bring the supply to 0 V, slide in the 100 Ω module, and work its set-points, which end at 5.5 V.

In Part IV the graph plots your points with a best-fit line through (0, 0). The slope is your resistance.

The one equation

Ohm’s law, both directions:

  • V = R·I and R = V / I

Plot V vertical against I horizontal and a fixed resistor draws a straight line through the origin.

The slope of that line is the resistance, in ohms: slope = ΔV / ΔI.

Why the line starts at (0, 0)

At zero volts the current is zero, so the line owns the origin.

A small offset away from zero usually comes from the leads and the meter contacts, and the bench tells you that out loud.

The honest limits

Real carbon resistors warm up and drift by 1 to 2 %, so the points do not sit exactly on the line.

Reading an analog face from an angle gives about ±1 mA of parallax.

The ammeter’s lead resistance pushes the slope a little low, and the voltmeter’s loading pushes it a little high. Both are below this bench’s resolution, and the bench names them instead of hiding them.

The 100 Ω run stops at 5.5 V because 11 V would give 110 mA, past the 100 mA stop. That is a meter-range artefact, not physics.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Wiring the ammeter in parallel, where it pegs or burns.
  • Dialing 11 V across the 100 Ω module and closing the key.
  • Swapping volts and amperes on the graph, so the slope is not R.
  • Swapping the resistor while the supply is still dialed up.
  • Writing 0.040 A as 0.040 mA in the milliamp column.

Part I — Wire the circuit

  1. Drag the red lead from the tray to the supply + terminal, then drag it on to the ammeter in terminal.
  2. Drag a black lead from the ammeter out terminal to the resistor left terminal, and another from the resistor right terminal back to supply -.
  3. Drag the red lead to the voltmeter + terminal and on to the resistor left terminal.
  4. Drag the black lead from the voltmeter - terminal to the resistor right terminal.
  5. Press "Check wiring". The bench names every missing lead and every wrong one.
  6. Click a laid lead to send it back to the tray. The meters stay dark until the wiring passes.

Part II — The 200 ohm run

  1. Press "Insert 200 ohm". The module slides into the bay with its nominal printed on it.
  2. Dial the supply to 2 V. Close the tap key and read the ammeter in mA and the voltmeter in V.
  3. Press "Log point". The row lands in the table and the point lands on the graph.
  4. Open the key, dial the next set-point, close, read, log. Work 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 11 V.
  5. The (0, 0) row is already on the table: zero volts means zero current.
  6. Bring the supply back to 0 V before you touch the resistor bay.

Part III — The 100 ohm run

  1. Check that the supply reads 0 V, then press "Insert 100 ohm". The bay refuses to swap at voltage.
  2. Dial 1 V, close the key, read, and log the point.
  3. Work 2, 3, 4 and 5 V the same way.
  4. At 5.5 V the ammeter is still under the stop. Log it, and the printed run ends.
  5. Dial higher and close the key anyway: the needle pegs past the 100 mA stop and the bench refuses the point.
  6. That is the meter-range artefact the Learn tab names: the cap is the ammeter, not the physics.

Part IV — Read the slope

  1. The graph plots both runs: volts vertical, amperes horizontal, both lines through (0, 0).
  2. The best-fit line draws itself as you log points, forced through the origin.
  3. Read the slope under the graph for each run, against its nominal value.
  4. Work the percent error: |slope minus nominal| / nominal times 100.
  5. In Fun Mode the modules warm up by up to 2 % and the faces carry ±1 mA parallax, so the line is not perfect.
  6. The nominal, the slope, and the percent error print below each data table.

The sim’s own props. The drag-wire builder uses the jack-to-jack wiring of the standard bench as the sim’s own setup: supply, ammeter, resistor bay, voltmeter, and the tap key on the return wire. The instructor-check step is the auto-check button.
