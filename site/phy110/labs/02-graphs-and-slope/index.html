Lab 2 · Graph scales, plotting, and slope

Build an axis you can read, put five points on the paper, draw one straight line, and take its slope. In this course the slope is often the answer: on a speed-time graph it is g itself.

Words in this lab

  • axis — one numbered side of the graph, x or y.
  • division — one gap between two printed marks.
  • scale — what one division is worth, in units.
  • origin — the value the axis starts at.
  • plot — a mark showing one measured pair of numbers.
  • trendline — one straight line that suits all the plots.
  • slope — rise divided by run along that line.
  • coverage — how much of the axis your data fills.

What you do at the bench

You get paper with a set number of divisions, and a small table of numbers. You decide what each division is worth, where each axis starts, and where the points go. Then you lay a ruler on the points, draw one line, and read its slope. The rest of the semester is built on that slope.

Picking a scale in three steps

Call the smallest value XS, the largest XL, and the number of divisions you have N. First find the pre-scale:

  • PS = (XL − XS) / N

Now pick the scale S from three friendly numbers only: 1, 2, and 5, moved by powers of ten. Take the smallest one that still spans your data. If PS comes out 1.33, the answer is 2. If PS comes out 5.83, the answer is 10. If PS is exactly 2, you keep 2.

Never pick 3 or 7 as a scale. Marks land in awkward places, and every reader of your graph pays for it.

Where the axis may start

The axis does not have to begin at zero. Start it where it reads best, but every point must stay on the paper. The class formula for the start is:

  • Xc = XS − (N × S − (XL − XS)) / 2

Then you may nudge Xc to a rounder number, as long as the last point still fits. If nudging pushes a point off the paper, the nudge is not allowed.

One more rule bites people: your data must cover at least 40% of the axis length. A tight little clump of five dots in one corner tells the reader nothing about the slope.

The trendline and its slope

Draw one straight line that suits all the points. Do not join the dots with segments; a zigzag line has no slope at all. Good lines leave about the same number of points above as below, and never leave all the points on one side.

Then pick two points on the line, far apart, and do the arithmetic:

  • slope = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1)

Those two points come from the line, not from your data. A data point usually sits a little off the line, so using it drags the slope around. This one habit is why two students with the same dots report different g.

What a slope is worth

A slope is never just a number. It carries units and a meaning. On a speed-time graph the slope is an acceleration, so the falling-ball table hands you the local strength of gravity. On a force-versus-stretch graph the slope is the stiffness of the spring. Say what your slope measures, in the same sentence as its number.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • No title, or axis names with no units in brackets.
  • A scale like 3 per division, which makes every mark awkward.
  • Data that fills a fifth of the paper because the axis is too grand.
  • Joining the dots instead of drawing one line through them.
  • Taking the slope from two data points, often the two nearest ones.
  • Quoting the slope with no units and no statement of what it measures.

Bench A - Read values off a plotted graph

  1. Pick a data set from the list.
  2. Move the pointer over the graph to park the crosshair.
  3. Read where the guides hit each axis.
  4. Press "Read one division" to zoom the axis strip below.
  5. Count the divisions in the strip and divide the interval by them.
  6. Press "Log this reading" to keep the pair you read.

  1. Pick the data set you are plotting in Bench A.

  3. Drag the two line handles until the line suits all five dots.

  5. Press "Snap to balanced" if you want to see the honest reference line.

Numbers this bench uses

Course rules, printed in the manual:

  • Scale steps come from 1, 2, 5, with 10 as the top bracket.
  • Data must cover at least 40% of the axis length.
  • A title, axis names with units, and axis values are mandatory.
  • Never join the dots with segments.
  • The accepted values are g = 9.80 m/s² on Earth and 1.62 m/s² on the Moon.
