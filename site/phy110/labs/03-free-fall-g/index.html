Lab 3 · Free fall: measuring g

Drop a steel ball from three set heights, time each fall on a four-decimal timer, and pull g out of your own averages. The bench grades you against 9.80 m/s², and your number is allowed to come out somewhere else.

Words in this lab

  • free fall — falling with only gravity pulling. The air drag here is too small to matter.
  • g — the pull of gravity near the ground. This course accepts 9.80 m/s².
  • trial — one drop and one timing. You do five per height.
  • average — add the numbers, divide by how many.
  • percent error — the gap from 9.80, written as a percent.
  • release — the clip that holds the ball, then opens on one click.

What you do at the bench

The bench has a stand with a sliding release, a steel ball, a landing pad, a meter stick, and a timer that reads four decimals. You pick a set height, lock the release, and drop the ball five times.

You time every fall, average the five, and pull g out of that average. Then you repeat at two more heights. The last line of the table is the lab answer: the average of your three g values.

The one equation that matters

A drop from rest obeys one line. The distance h it falls in a time t is

  • h = g·t² / 2

Turn it around and gravity is on its own:

  • g = 2h / t²

Put h in metres and t in seconds, and g comes out in m/s². The whole lab is that division, done once per height.

Reading the height the honest way

The meter stick reads from the pad top up to the ball bottom. The fall ends when the ball bottom meets the pad top, so that is the true distance.

Read to the ball centre instead and every height is half a ball too long. Here that is 1 cm, and every g you compute comes out a little too high. The rule is printed on the bench: bottom to top.

Why your g comes out a little wrong

The timer starts and stops by itself, so the slips are small. The two that matter are the release and the height geometry, and both sit in the millisecond range.

The same slip hurts more on a short fall. From 0.200 m the fall takes about 0.20 s, so a 3 ms slip is 1.5% of the time. From 0.800 m it is under 0.4%. The tall drop is the honest one.

A good class result lands between 9.3 and 10.1 m/s². If every one of your drops prints exactly 9.80, something is being smoothed, not measured.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Reading the height to the ball centre. Every height grows by half a ball and g comes out high.
  • Forgetting the square on t, or dividing by t the wrong way around.
  • Averaging the g values before averaging the times. The data page averages the times first.
  • Logging a trial under the wrong height column.
  • Writing 9.80 in the report because it is expected, when your drops say otherwise.

Bench A - Drop, time, average

  1. Drag the release clip along the rod, or click a set height: 0.200, 0.400, or 0.800 m.
  2. Press "Lock release" when the clip sits where you want it.
  3. Press "Load ball" to put the steel ball into the clip.
  4. Check the sight from above: the pad snaps under the ball, on the switch.
  5. Read the height on the meter stick, from the ball bottom to the pad top.
  6. Press "Release". The timer starts electrically and stops at the pad.
  7. The time lands in trial 1 of 5. Press "Load ball" and drop four more times.
  8. Do the same at the other two heights. The footer rows do the averages.

Drag this — the release clip slides up and down the rod. Grab it to move it; let go and it stays where you left it.

The fall animation is slowed so you can see it. The timer keeps true time.

Numbers this bench uses

Course values, printed in the manual:

  • Accepted g = 9.80 m/s². The percent error is the gap from this value.
  • Timer resolution 0.0001 s, with Reset and Recall of the last five times.
  • Set heights 0.200, 0.400, 0.800 m. Five timed trials per height.
  • Average the times per height, then average the three g values.
  • G = 6.67×10-11 N·m²/kg², for the post-lab model g = G·ME / (RE + h)².

Not printed in the manual, shown here so nothing is a guess:

  • ME = 5.97×1024 kg and RE = 6.37×106 m, the standard Earth mass and radius used in that model.

The sim's own props. Steel ball 2 cm across; the pad snaps directly under the ball, on its switch.

Fun Mode noise. Drop time ±4 ms, the size of a real release and geometry slip. Fun Mode never changes the accepted 9.80.

Honest limits. A good real class result is 9.3 to 10.1 m/s². Drag on a steel ball over these drops is negligible, so this sim does not add drag noise. The dominant slips are release timing and height geometry, in milliseconds, so that is what Fun Mode jitters.
