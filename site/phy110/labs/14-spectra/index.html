Lab 14 · Line and continuous spectra with a spectroscope

Look at lamp light through a grating spectroscope, plot where the lines fall against the wavelength scale, and match each gas to its fingerprint.

Words in this lab

  • spectrum — light spread out into its colours, by wavelength.
  • wavelength — the distance from one crest of a light wave to the next, in nanometres (nm).
  • continuous spectrum — an unbroken band of every colour, from a hot solid.
  • line spectrum — bright thin lines on a dark ground, from a hot thin gas.
  • spectroscope — a tool with a slit and a grating that spreads light into its spectrum.
  • fingerprint — the pattern of an element’s lines. No two elements share it.
  • ultraviolet — light below 380 nm, which your eye cannot see.

What you do at the bench

The rack holds seven sources: a filament bulb, a fluorescent tube, hydrogen, helium, neon, mercury and argon.

A lab jack on the lamp stand raises the chosen source into the slit’s line of sight.

You hold the spectroscope to your eye and rotate it to sweep the spectrum.

Below the view, a recording chart carries one strip per source on a 380–700 nm scale.

You click each line you see onto its strip, by colour and position.

A reference chart toggle lays the known lines over the strips, for the identification work.

The one equation

  • E = h·f and c = f·λ — one drop between atomic levels emits one wavelength. This level framing is standard; the manual keeps the idea qualitative and does not print the equation.
  • A grating spreads the light by diffraction, a prism by refraction, and several diffraction orders can exist.

What the view shows

A hot solid, the filament, gives the whole band at once.

A hot thin gas gives only its own lines, and only those.

The fluorescent tube mixes both: bright mercury lines on a faint continuous background.

This view is bright and clean. The real one is dim and sensitive to the room light.

The honest limits

The manual is internally inconsistent here. Its equipment list names an argon lamp, and its procedure says to view mercury. This bench ships both, and the mismatch is footnoted instead of quietly picked over.

An uncalibrated handheld scope gives line positions, not certified wavelengths, so grading is colour plus band with a ±30 nm tolerance, never exact.

The line brightness order is not graded. It changes with lamp age.

The colour-band figure in the manual carries only the letters V B G Y O R. The band edges here are the sim’s own.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Calling the fluorescent tube a pure line source. The faint band is part of it.
  • Grading a line by its exact number instead of its colour and band.
  • Plotting a line where you expect it, not where you saw it.
  • Mixing up which strip is which source on the chart.

Part 1 — View the seven sources

  1. Pick a source from the rack: filament, He, H, Hg, Ar, Ne, or fluorescent.
  2. Press "Jack up". The source enters the slit line of sight.
  3. Drag across the scope view to sweep the spectrum.
  4. Note where the bright lines sit, their colours, and the scale below.
  5. Click the source’s chart strip at each line you saw. A mark plots there.
  6. Turn on the reference chart to compare your marks with the known lines.
  7. Repeat for every source, and draw in the filament’s continuous band.

Part 2 — Identify an unknown gas

  1. Press "Load unknown". An unnamed gas comes up in the slit.
  2. Jack it up and sweep the view. Note the lines and their colours.
  3. Plot those lines on the chart strip you think they belong to.
  4. Turn on the reference chart and find the strip your marks line up with.
  5. Pick "I think it is..." from the five gases.
  6. Press "Record ID". The bench checks your marks against that gas’s lines.

Numbers this bench uses

Course values, printed in the manual:

  • Visible range about 380 to 760 nm.
  • Line sources give discrete lines; hot solids give a continuous band.

Not printed in the manual, shown here so nothing is a guess:

  • Colour-band edges 380 / 450 / 485 / 500 / 565 / 590 / 700 nm, the sim’s own build of the figure’s letters V B G Y O R.
  • The line reference set: standard tabulated values (NIST), shipped as the sim’s own answer key. H 656.3, 486.1, 434.0; He 667.8, 587.6, 501.6, 471.3, 447.1; Hg 579.0, 577.0, 546.1, 435.8; Ar 750.4, 706.7, 696.5, 496.5, 430.1, 420.1; Ne 650.7, 640.8, 614.3, 603.0, 585.2 nm.
  • Grating lines per millimetre. The chart reports positions, so no grating number is needed.

The manual’s mismatch, footnoted. The equipment list names an argon lamp; the procedure says to view mercury. This bench ships both lamps and never silently picks one.

The sim’s own setup. The recording chart’s exact layout is image-only, so the seven strips on the 380–700 nm scale are the sim’s own standard build. The scope view is bright and clean; the real view is dim and sensitive to room light, so this one is idealised.

Fun Mode noise. Every line position wobbles by a stable ±30 nm, the uncalibrated-scope honesty. Fun Mode never changes the reference set, the visible range, or any accepted value, and the line brightness order is never graded.
