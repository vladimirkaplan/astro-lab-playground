Lab 4 · Atwood’s machine: Newton’s second law

Build a slightly unbalanced pair of hangers, time the fall five times, and compare your measured acceleration with the theory value that includes the pulley. The bench grades you against its own theory line, and your number is allowed to come out somewhere else.

Words in this lab

  • Atwood’s machine — a pulley hung from a ceiling, with two masses on one string. One side goes up as the other goes down.
  • unbalanced — the two sides do not weigh the same. The heavier side pulls.
  • acceleration — how fast the speed builds up, in m/s². This lab measures it two ways.
  • pulley mass — the pulley is not massless. It is a solid disc that spins up as the sides move.
  • trial — one mass setup with five timed drops.
  • h — the drop, read from the holder bottom to the floor.
  • percent difference — how far your measured a sits from the theory a, written as a percent.

What you do at the bench

The bench has a ceiling pulley, two weight holders above a floor line, a tray of slotted weights, a meter stick, and a stopwatch.

You type the pulley mass and the two holder masses once. On the real machine those numbers are printed on the parts.

You drag slotted weights onto the holders until one side is a little heavier, then drag the heavy holder to a release height. The meter stick reads h from the holder bottom to the floor.

You start the stopwatch, release the holders, and the clock stops at the floor. You do that five times, average the five times, and pull a out of the average. Four trials finish the data page.

The one equation that matters

The pull on the system is the mass difference. The inertia is all the mass, with the pulley counting as half. So the theory value is

  • a = (m2 − m1)·g / (m1 + m2 + M/2)

The measured side is one line too, from the fall of h in a time t:

  • a = 2h / t²

You may keep every mass in grams. The ratio cancels the units, so no kg conversion is needed.

Why the pulley counts as half

The pulley is a solid disc, and it must spin up as the string moves. For a uniform disc, that spin-up effort works out to half its mass.

So the denominator carries M/2 and not the whole pulley mass. Drop the pulley from the formula and a comes out about 11 % too big on the course example.

Reading h the honest way

The meter stick reads from the holder bottom to the floor. That is the distance the fall really covers, so that is the h you use.

Reading from the holder top or its centre makes h wrong by part of a holder, and every a you compute comes out with it.

Why your a comes out a little low

A real pulley has friction in its bearing, and its string is not massless. Both pull the measured a below the theory value, by a few percent.

Your hand on the stopwatch is not perfect either. That is why the bench asks for five repeats: the average spreads a fast or slow hand across five drops instead of one.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Putting the whole pulley mass M in the formula instead of M/2. That pushes a up by about 11 %.
  • A mass difference over 20 g. The fall gets fast and the timing gets rough.
  • Starting the stopwatch after you release the holders.
  • Averaging the a values instead of the five times first.
  • Switching one mass to kg and leaving the rest in grams.

Bench A — Set up, drop, time

  1. Type the pulley mass and the two holder masses. They are printed on the real machine.
  2. Drag slotted weights from the tray onto the holders, or press a course-trial button.
  3. Drag the heavy holder up or down. The meter stick reads h from the holder bottom to the floor.
  4. Press Start, then Release as fast as you can. The fall runs at real speed.
  5. The clock stops at the floor hit. Repeat until the trial has five timed drops.
  6. Press Average 5 to pull a_measured out of the mean time, then Next trial.
  7. Four trials finish the table. The percent-difference row uses the mean as its denominator, and it says so.

Drag this — slotted weights move between the tray and the holders; the heavy holder moves up and down to set the release height h.

The fall animates at real speed, so a 2.6 s drop takes 2.6 s. The stopwatch keeps true time.

Numbers this bench uses

Course values, printed in the manual:

  • g = 9.80 m/s². Four trials, five timed drops each.
  • Trial 1 differs by about 10 g between the holders; trials 2–4 add about 20 g to each holder, so the difference stays 10 g.
  • Mass-difference rule: keep |m2 − m1| at 20 g or less, so the fall stays slow enough to time.
  • Grams are allowed throughout: the theory line is one ratio, so the units cancel.
  • The percent difference uses the mean of measured and theory as its denominator, printed here and on the table.

Not printed in the manual, shown here so nothing is a guess:

  • Holder mass 50 g each, pulley mass 70 g. On the real machine those numbers are stamped on the parts, not printed in the manual.

The sim’s own props. Slotted weights come in 100, 50, 20 and 10 g. Release height runs 0.30–1.00 m. The fall animates at real speed.

Fun Mode noise. Each trial the instrument loses 2–8 % of the drive (bearing friction plus string), and the stop button slips up to 0.05 s per drop. Fun Mode never changes g = 9.80 or any course value.

Honest limits. On a real rig the measured a usually lands a few percent below theory. A big mass difference makes the fall fast and the timing rough, which is what the course rule is for. Real kits often have two holders of unequal mass, so type each one separately.
