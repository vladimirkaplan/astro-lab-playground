Lab 8 · Specific heat of a metal by mixtures

Weigh the metal, the cup and the water. Boil the metal, drop it into the calorimeter, stir, and read the final temperature. The energy balance gives the metal’s specific heat, and the table of accepted values names the metal. The stamp on the cylinder stays hidden until you have measured it.

Words in this lab

  • specific heat c — the heat that warms 1 g of a material by 1 °C. In cal/g·°C.
  • energy balance — the line that says what is lost equals what is gained.
  • calorimeter — the insulating cup that holds the mix water.
  • Tₓ — the starting temperature of the hot metal. Here, the boiling-water temperature.
  • Tᵢ — the temperature of the cup water before the mix.
  • TḬ — the temperature after the mix, once stirring has settled it.
  • stirrer — the rod you turn so the whole cup settles to one temperature.

What you do at the bench

The bench has a water boiler and a calorimeter cup in an insulating jacket. A thermometer probe drags between the two. A triple-beam balance weighs the items on a shelf.

In Part I you weigh three things, one at a time: the dry metal cylinder, the empty cup, and the cup with water. Each reading is one number in the table.

In Part II you turn on the boiler and wait for the boiling indicator. Then you let the cylinder sit in the boiling water until it reaches Tₓ.

In Part III you read Tᵢ with the probe in the cup. Then you transfer the cylinder while the timer runs. You stir until the temperature settles, and then read TḬ.

The bench then writes the eleven-row sheet, computes your cₓ, and reveals the stamp on the cylinder.

The equations

The heat gained or lost by a material is

  • Q = c · m · (T₂ − T₁)

In the mix, what the metal loses equals what the cup and the water gain:

  • cₓ mₓ (Tₓ − TḬ) = (m₏ c₏ + mw cw) (TḬ − Tᵢ)

Solved for the unknown specific heat, the working form is

  • cₓ = (m₏ c₏ + mw cw) (TḬ − Tᵢ) / [mₓ (Tₓ − TḬ)]

Course units: c in cal/g·°C, mass in grams, temperature in °C.

Tₓ is 100 °C, the boiling-water temperature at 1 atm. The manual states no pressure, so the course assumes 100 °C and labels it.

The percent error is |cₓ − accepted| / accepted × 100.

Why your number will sit a few percent off

An ideal mix loses nothing. The cup and the jacket hold the heat in.

A real mix leaks a little heat to the room, and a drop or two of water splashes out on the transfer.

So a measured cₓ a few percent off the table line is an honest result, not a failed one.

Boiling water that rides along on the cylinder adds heat the line does not count, and pushes your cₓ high.

A cylinder that arrives cooler than Tₓ makes your cₓ read low. The gap (Tₓ − TḬ) you use is then bigger than the metal’s true drop.

The honest limits

The balance reads to 0.05 g and the thermometer to 0.1 °C. That is the precision ceiling of the bench.

TḬ peaks and then cools, so the timing of the reading matters.

The manual assumes c is constant over 25 to 100 °C. That is fair over this span.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Reading TḬ before the stirrer has settled the cup.
  • Using m₏ + mw where the line wants mw alone.
  • Taking Tₓ from the cup water instead of from the boiling water.
  • Swapping (TḬ − Tᵢ) and (Tₓ − TḬ) in the line.
  • Dropping the cup’s own share, m₏ c₏, from the left side.

Part I — Weigh the three masses

  1. Pick the metal cylinder you want to work with. Its stamp reads X until you have measured it.
  2. Drag the dry metal cylinder from the shelf onto the balance pan. The readout is m_s, the metal mass.
  3. Drag it off the pan, then drag the empty calorimeter cup on. The readout is m_c.
  4. Drag the cup off, then drag the cup with water on. The readout is m_c + m_w.
  5. Subtract m_c from m_c + m_w and the sheet gives m_w, the water mass.
  6. Note that the balance reads to 0.05 g, like the real triple-beam.

Part II — Bring the metal to T_s

  1. Turn the boiler on and wait for the boiling indicator.
  2. Drag the metal cylinder from the shelf into the boiling water.
  3. Wait for the "cylinder at 100 C" note. The metal now sits at T_s.
  4. Drag the thermometer probe to the boiler to check the temperature if you like.
  5. Do not transfer yet: the cup water still needs its T_i reading.

Part III — Mix, stir, and read T_f

  1. Drag the probe into the calorimeter. The first reading in the cup is T_i, and it locks in.
  2. Press "Transfer now". The timer counts the transfer, and the cylinder lands in the cup.
  3. Turn the stirrer on and let it run for a couple of seconds until the temperature settles.
  4. Only then does the probe read T_f. Stir first, or the reading stays locked.
  5. Press "Log the run". The bench writes the eleven-row sheet, computes c_s, and reveals the stamp.
  6. Compare your c_s with the accepted value and the percent error, then "New run" if you want another metal.

Drag this — the shelf items move to the pan and the boiler, and the probe moves between the docks. Drop an item back on the shelf to take it off the pan.

The sim’s own props. The boiler holds enough water for the cylinder to sit fully under. The calorimeter is the standard styrofoam-cup jacket with lid and stirrer; the lid and jacket are the sim’s own setup, drawn from the procedure text. The probe docks at rest, over the boiler, or in the calorimeter.

Fun Mode noise. The balance reads ±0.05 g, the thermometer ±0.1 °C, the transfer leaks up to about 30 cal to the room, and up to 0.4 g of boiling water rides along on the cylinder. Together they move cₓ by a few percent, like the real bench. Fun Mode never changes the c values, the masses, or any course value.

Honest limits. A real calorimeter is not isolated: heat loss and splash carry cₓ a few percent off, and carry-over water and condensation push it upward. Cooling corrections are out of scope at this level, and the manual assumes c is constant with temperature, which is fair over 25 to 100 °C.
