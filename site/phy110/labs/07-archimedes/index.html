Lab 7 · Buoyant force and Archimedes’ principle

Lower a hooked cylinder into water, read how much the level rises, then hang it submerged from a tared beaker on the balance. The two routes must give the same buoyant force. The cylinder reads to about 1 mL, so an honest lab lands a double-digit percent difference, and that is the point.

Words in this lab

  • buoyant force — the up push of water on a submerged object. In newtons.
  • displaced volume Vᵢ — the volume of water the object pushes aside.
  • apparent weight — what the scale reads while the object hangs in the water. Smaller than the true weight.
  • triple-beam balance — a scale with sliding riders that reads grams.
  • graduated cylinder — the 50 mL tube with tick marks for reading water levels.
  • meniscus — the curve of the water surface. Read at its bottom edge.
  • tare — zero the scale with something on the pan, so only what you add counts.
  • newton — the unit of force. The weight of about 102 g at the ground.

What you do at the bench

The bench has a 50 mL graduated cylinder, a 250 mL beaker, a triple-beam balance, and three hooked metal cylinders stamped Cu, T and Z.

In Part I you pour water into the cylinder, read the level, lower a hooked cylinder on a thin wire, and read the level again. The rise is the object volume.

In Part II you weigh the dry cylinder in air. Then you set a quarter-full beaker on the balance pan, tare it, hang the cylinder fully submerged from a wire, and read the apparent mass.

In Part III you log the metal. The bench writes both routes and the percent difference between them.

The one equation

Water pushes up on anything that takes its place. The size of the push is

  • Fₚ = ρᵢ · Vᵢ · g

Fully submerged, the displaced volume Vᵢ equals the object volume V₄. So the test line of the lab is

  • Fₘ − Fₘ(apparent) = ρᵢ · V₄ · g

If the two sides agree within the bench error, the principle holds for that metal.

Units: ρᵢ in kg/m³, volume in m³, g = 9.80 m/s², force in newtons. One reminder: 1 mL = 1 cm³ = 1×10⁻³ m³.

Why the two routes should agree

The volume route measures how much water the object pushed aside.

The weight route measures how much the water pushed the object up.

Archimedes says these are the same thing, so the two Fₚ numbers should land close together.

Coarse water, honest percent

A real 50 mL cylinder reads to about 1 mL. Your object is about 2.5 mL. So the volume route carries a roughly 20 % slip, and double-digit percent differences between the routes are the normal bench result.

Bubbles stuck to the metal and the wire’s own volume skew the level by about 0.1 mL. Tap out the bubbles before you read.

Tap water sits about 1 % off from 1.00 g/cm³. At this precision that is nothing, and the bench says so out loud.

Air buoyancy is ignored, as the course says.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Reading the top of the meniscus instead of its bottom edge.
  • Reading the level before the cylinder is fully under the water.
  • Skipping the tare, so the beaker and water ride in the reading.
  • Mixing mL and m³ in the Fₚ line. 2.5 mL is 2.5×10⁻³ m³, not 2.5.
  • Comparing the routes before both are logged.

Part I — Volume route: displace and read the level

  1. Pick the metal you will work on. Its hooked cylinder lifts off the shelf.
  2. Set the pour slider near half the cylinder, about 25 mL. Read V_water at the bottom of the meniscus.
  3. Press "Lower into cylinder" so the hooked cylinder drops on its thin wire until it is fully under the water.
  4. Read the new level, V_water+cyl. The rise, V_water+cyl minus V_water, is the object volume V_o.
  5. If bubbles stick to the metal, press "Tap out the bubbles" and read again.
  6. Note that the bench reads the level to about 1 mL, like a real 50 mL cylinder.

Part II — Weight route: weigh dry, then submerged

  1. Press "Weigh in air". The dry cylinder sits on the pan and the readout gives the mass in grams, with the kg cell beside it.
  2. Press "Beaker on pan" to move the balance to the quarter-full 250 mL beaker.
  3. Press "Tare pan". The beaker and its water now weigh zero on the scale.
  4. Press "Lower into beaker" so the cylinder hangs fully submerged from the wire over the beaker.
  5. The readout is the apparent mass, in grams and kilograms.
  6. Press "Raise from beaker" before you switch to the next metal.

Part III — Record the metal and compare the routes

  1. Make sure you have a V_water+cyl reading from Part I and an apparent-mass reading from Part II.
  2. Press "Log this metal". The bench writes one row in each table.
  3. Table 1 is the volume route: V_o in mL and m³, then Fₚ = 1000 × V₄ × 9.80.
  4. Table 2 is the weight route: Fₘ, Fₘ apparent, and their difference as Fₚ.
  5. The footer compares your two Fₚ numbers with the percent difference, and it names the accepted Fₚ out loud.
  6. Repeat for the other two metals. Three rows per table finish the sheet.
