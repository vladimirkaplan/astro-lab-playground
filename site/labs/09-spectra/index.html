Lab 9 · Spectra

A bench spectroscope, five gas tubes, two kinds of lamp, and the reason every astronomer loves diffraction gratings: light carries the fingerprint of whatever made it.

What this lab is about

You hold a diffraction-grating spectroscope up to a row of light sources: an incandescent bulb, tubes of glowing hydrogen, helium, neon, mercury, krypton, and a fluorescent lamp. Each one paints a different picture across the viewport. Your job is to describe the pictures, record where the bright lines sit, and then use those line patterns as fingerprints: match unknown spectra to elements and say what lines in a star spectrum would tell an astronomer.

The physics: three kinds of spectra

Continuous. A hot, dense object - a bulb filament, a star's visible surface - glows at every visible wavelength at once. The rainbow runs unbroken from red to violet, and its balance of colors tracks temperature: hotter looks bluer.

Emission lines. A hot, thin gas is different. Its atoms can only hold certain electron energies, so the light they emit comes only at certain wavelengths - a few colored hairlines on a black field. The set of wavelengths is fixed by the element's electron structure, so every element gets a unique pattern, as individual as a fingerprint.

Absorption lines. Run a continuous glow through a cooler gas and the gas removes exactly the wavelengths it would itself emit, punching dark lines into the rainbow. Same fingerprint, flipped to negative. (This lab views continuous and emission spectra; the star question previews absorption.)

How the viewer works. The grating is a slide ruled with thousands of fine grooves. Light waves leaving neighboring grooves interfere, and the direction where they reinforce depends on wavelength, so different colors leave at different angles. One consequence people get tripped by: a grating bends red the most. A glass prism does the opposite and bends violet the most. If a write-up claims violet was "bent most" while using a grating viewer, the author was thinking of a prism.

What you will actually see

Hydrogen shows just four visible lines: one red, one blue-green, two violet (the Balmer series). Helium adds more lines with a prominent yellow one. Neon blankets the orange-red - that is neon-sign color. Mercury has a fierce blue-green line and a yellow pair. Krypton pairs a strong violet-blue duo with an orange cluster. The fluorescent lamp is the mixed case: a coat of white phosphor glows in broad bands while mercury vapor inside contributes sharp lines - continuous and emission at once.

Why this matters to astronomers: spectra are the one place astronomy can touch matter it will never visit. Line patterns say which elements are in a star or cloud; the exact pattern says it is hydrogen and not something that merely looks reddish; and their wholesale shift toward red measures how fast the source recedes, which is exactly how the expansion of the universe (Lab 15) was discovered. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Sketching a blend of all the colors for a gas tube. Gases show separated lines, not paint.
  • Recording the color but not a wavelength position; the grating has a scale - use it.
  • Confusing the grating's bend order (red most) with the prism's (violet most).
  • Calling the fluorescent lamp "continuous" and missing mercury's green line sitting on top.
  • Matching an unknown by overall tint instead of the exact set of line positions.
  • Assuming unseen lines are absent: ultraviolet and infrared lines are real and measurable.
