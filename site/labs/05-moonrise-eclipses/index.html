Lab 5 · Moonrise drift and the Eclipse Board

New moon every 29.5 days, full moon every 29.5 days - and yet no eclipse last month and probably none next month. This board shows the one detail the flat diagrams hide: the Moon travels a tilted road, and eclipses only happen where that road crosses the Sun's road. The lab's other half is a question you can ask with no eclipse at all: the Moon is late to rise a little more every night, and this page chases that delay too.

What this lab is about

Draw the classic top-view diagram and eclipses should happen every month: new moon between Earth and Sun means shadow on the ground, full moon means the Moon in Earth's shadow. The diagram is lying by omission. It flattens the sky onto a sheet of paper, and the one fact that decides eclipses is vertical: the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5.1 degrees to Earth's orbital road (the ecliptic), the road the Sun appears to walk.

Two roads, two crossings, two shadow windows

  • The crossing points are the nodes. The tilted lunar orbit pierces the flat road at exactly two points, opposite each other. Only there can the three bodies stack in true line.
  • Eclipse season: when the Sun sits near the node line (this board uses a +-16 degree model window, a course-style approximation), the geometry is open for roughly a month. Any new moon in the window makes a solar eclipse; the full moon about two weeks later, sitting at the opposite node, makes a lunar one.
  • Outside the window, the miss is big. Most full moons the side view would place the Moon about 5 degrees above or below the ecliptic - night by daylight because that is many times wider than Earth's shadow reads at the Moon (well under one degree). Most new moons, the little Moon-shadow cone sweeps north or south of the whole planet.
  • Within a season, the Moon's exact height decides the type. Nearly on the line: central (total or annular) solar, or a deep umbral lunar eclipse. A little off: partial. Only grazing the outer shadow band: penumbral - faint enough that you often need a camera to notice.

The 2027 board to the right carries NASA's verified events: an annular solar eclipse Feb 6 with a penumbral lunar on Feb 20 (one season), then a penumbral graze Jul 18, a total solar eclipse Aug 2 with a penumbral lunar Aug 17 (the next season). Watch the node line point at the Sun exactly when the dots light up. Same clock, one lopsided dial: the seasons drift earlier by about 19 days each year.

One more honesty note the board keeps reminding you of: the sim never predicts eclipse types. Their geometry comes from real ephemeris data and the types are copied from the NASA canon table - the sim shows you *why* those dates are the dates.

Why this matters to astronomers: the node geometry is the oldest precision astronomy there is - Babylonian saros cycles and Han-dynasty eclipse records both map this same tide of seasons. Today the same three numbers (phase, distance to node, apparent shadow width) still drive mission planning from Mars surface operations to telescope scheduling. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Saying new moon causes solar eclipses. New moons are the necessary shift; the alignment is the job.
  • Blaming clouds, or Earth's distance, for the miss.
  • Calling a penumbral lunar eclipse "no eclipse" because it looked fine.
  • Forgetting that during a given eclipse season, the lunar and solar eclipses happen about a fortnight apart.
  • Reading the sim's shadow verdicts as predictions. Types are NASA facts baked into the table, and the board says so.
  • Drawing the node line pointing wherever. The two solar events of a year pin the node line to the Sun's longitude on those dates.

The other half: why moonrise is late (and getting later)

Forget eclipses for a moment and just watch the Moon rise. On any clear stretch you notice the same thing: the Moon comes up a little later every night, and the delay is close to 50 minutes. That number is not a fact about the Moon's brightness or distance - it is a race between two spins. Earth turns once under the stars in about 24 hours, but the Moon is crawling eastward along its orbit the whole time, about 12 degrees a day. To catch the Moon on the eastern horizon again, Earth has to swing that extra 12 degrees around, and 12 degrees takes about 50 minutes. So a Moonrise-to-Moonrise interval is really a lunar day of roughly 24 hours 50 minutes, and each night's moonrise slips later by that gap.

Why does the almanac disagree with a tidy 50, sometimes printing 40 one week and 70 the next? Two honest reasons, and this lab models only the second:

  • Season and tilt (named, not modeled here). The Moon's daily path meets the horizon at a shallow or steep angle depending on the season and on the ecliptic's tilt at our latitude. When the path is shallow, that same 12 degrees of eastward progress takes a long time to clear the horizon - this is the harvest moon, the run of small moonrise delays around the autumn full moon. Real day-to-day delays at New York latitude range roughly 40 to 70 minutes for this reason, and a mean-element model like this page's deliberately does not reproduce it.
  • The near/far swing (modeled here). The Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so its speed changes over the anomalistic month: near perigee it sweeps the sky faster, gains more than 12 degrees a day, and rises later than the 48.8-minute mean; near apogee it lags and rises a little earlier. This bench spans about 42 to 56 minutes and never claims to reach the ends of the 40-70 band.

The same ellipse that speeds the Moon up also changes how big it looks: closer means a wider disk. The mean apparent diameter is about 31 arcminutes - the width of a little fingernail at arm's length. This model swings between roughly 29.4 arcmin at apogee and 32.9 at its perigee; the loudest "supermoons" reach about 34 arcmin only because the real orbit's eccentricity itself breathes in and out, an effect the fixed-shape model names but does not chase.

How this ties to the eclipse board above: the Moon is late for exactly the reason it is usually not on the node line - it keeps moving east along a tilted orbit. An eclipse needs that moving road aimed so the node line points at the Sun (an eclipse season); moonrise lateness is the same eastward creep measured against the horizon instead of the Sun. One motion, two clocks. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 4, CC BY 4.0; the season-drift figure on the board above is untouched.)

Common mistakes with moonrise lateness:
  • Saying the Moon "rises late because it rises late." The 50 minutes is Earth turning through the Moon's daily eastward progress.
  • Blaming the 40-70 spread on the Moon's distance alone. Most of that spread is season and horizon tilt (the harvest moon), which this model leaves out on purpose.
  • Expecting 50.0 minutes on the bar. The mean is 48.8, and the near/far swing moves it a few minutes either way.
  • Calling a 34-arcmin moon "bigger than the model allows." Real eccentricity swings the near side a touch past this model's 32.9 - a named, unmodeled effect, not a bug.

Bench 3 - The 50-minute chase (moonrise drift)

This bench is the lab's other half and does not need an eclipse. Step the day through one mean lunar month (counted from a new moon by the moonrise model) and watch the moonrise hand walk around the 24-hour sky-clock while the disk cycles crescent → quarter → gibbous → full and back. The bar reports the modeled daily moonrise lag against the 48.8-minute mean, drawn on the honest 40-70 minute band so you can see how much of the real spread this simple model deliberately does not capture.
