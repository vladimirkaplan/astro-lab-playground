Lab 7 · Lenses and Telescopes

One lens makes images; two lenses make a telescope. Slide the parts, chase the sharpest spot, and let the thin-lens equation predict what your eyes see.

What this lab is about

On the bench you get a converging lens, a light source, and a white card. You move the card until the image snaps sharp, measure two distances, and test the only equation this lab needs: 1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i. Then you add a second lens, pull it to the right spacing, and the pair becomes a telescope.

The one equation

d_o is object-to-lens, d_i is lens-to-image, both in centimeters on your bench. A distant object (or the Sun) arrives with parallel light, so d_i = f exactly: focus an image of the Sun on paper and you have just measured the focal length of your lens. That is also why the sharp image of anything at infinity sits one focal length behind the lens.

  • d_o > f: real image - light actually converges, the card catches it, and the image is inverted. Camera, projector, your retina.
  • d_o < f: light never converges - the image is virtual, upright, and enlarged. That is a magnifying glass, and no card will ever catch that image.
  • Magnification m = -d_i/d_o. The minus sign IS the inversion; the size of m tells you how big. If m is a fraction, the image is smaller than the object.

The telescope

Point a long-focal-length objective at something far: its real image sits at f_objective, tiny and upside down. Now put a short-focal-length eyepiece so that this tiny image lands exactly at the eyepiece focal point: the escaping light is parallel again, relaxed-eye comfortable, and it arrives at your eye tilted f_objective/f_eyepiece times steeper than the naked beam. That ratio is the magnification, and the tube length is just f_objective + f_eyepiece.

Why buy binoculars instead of two spyglasses? The prisms inside flip the Keplerian upside-down picture right side up, and the overlapped light paths shorten the tube to something handheld. Astronomers happily keep the inverted view - orientation does not matter on the sky.

Why this matters to astronomers: every facility telescope is this two-lens idea scaled up, with mirrors instead of glass. The 19th century ruled the sky with refractors measured exactly the way you measure f here; and the sign of the game changed the day someone checked galaxies through a 100-inch version of the same geometry. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Flipping the reciprocals: f = d_o + d_i is never true; do the 1/x math.
  • Measuring distances from the lens mount edges, not lens to object/image.
  • Writing m as positive for a real image: that minus sign earns points.
  • Reporting magnification without "x" or a sign and letting the grader guess.
  • Declaring the virtual magnifier image "missing" because the card stays blurry forever.
  • Forgetting the inverted image when drawing the telescope's light path.
