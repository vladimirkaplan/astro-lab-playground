Lab 7 · Lenses and Telescopes

One lens makes images. Two lenses make a telescope. Slide the parts, chase the sharpest spot, and let the thin-lens equation predict what your eyes see.

Words in this lab

  • lens — shaped glass that bends light together or apart.
  • focal length (f) — the distance from a lens to the sharp point that parallel light reaches.
  • real image — an image that light really forms. A card can catch it.
  • virtual image — an image that seems to sit inside the lens. No card can catch it.
  • magnification — how many times bigger an image is than the object. Written with "x".
  • objective — the lens farthest from your eye. It gathers the light.
  • eyepiece — the lens closest to your eye. You look through it.

What this lab is about

On the bench you get a converging lens, a light source, and a white card. A converging lens bends parallel light inward, to a point. You move the card until the image snaps sharp. You measure two distances. Then you test the only equation this lab needs: 1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i. After that you add a second lens, pull it to the right spacing, and the pair becomes a telescope.

The one equation

Here d_o is the distance from the object to the lens. The d_i is the distance from the lens to the image. Both are in centimeters on your bench. A distant object, or the Sun, arrives with parallel light. So d_i = f, exactly. Focus an image of the Sun on paper and you have just measured the focal length of your lens. The focal length is the distance from the lens to the sharp point that parallel light reaches. That is also why the sharp image of anything at infinity sits one focal length behind the lens.

  • d_o > f: a real image. Light actually converges, the card catches it, and the image is inverted. Camera, projector, your retina.
  • d_o < f: light never converges. The image is virtual, upright, and enlarged. That is a magnifying glass, and no card will ever catch that image.
  • Magnification: m = -d_i/d_o. The minus sign IS the inversion. The size of m tells you how big. If m is a fraction, the image is smaller than the object.

The telescope

Point a long-focal-length objective at something far away. The objective is the lens farthest from your eye. Its real image sits at f_objective, tiny and upside down. Now put a short-focal-length eyepiece so that this tiny image lands exactly at the eyepiece focal point. The eyepiece is the lens you look through. The escaping light is parallel again, comfortable for a relaxed eye. It arrives at your eye tilted f_objective/f_eyepiece times steeper than the naked beam. That ratio is the magnification. The tube length is just f_objective + f_eyepiece.

Why buy binoculars instead of two spyglasses? The prisms inside flip the Keplerian upside-down picture right side up. And the overlapped light paths shorten the tube to something handheld. Astronomers happily keep the inverted view. Orientation does not matter on the sky.

Why this matters to astronomers: every facility telescope is this two-lens idea scaled up, with mirrors instead of glass. The 19th century ruled the sky with refractors. Their focal lengths were measured exactly the way you measure f here. And the sign of the game changed the day someone checked galaxies through a 100-inch version of the same geometry. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Flipping the reciprocals. f = d_o + d_i is never true; do the 1/x math.
  • Measuring distances from the lens mount edges, not lens to object or image.
  • Writing m as positive for a real image. That minus sign earns points.
  • Reporting magnification without "x" or a sign and letting the grader guess.
  • Declaring the virtual magnifier image "missing" because the card stays blurry forever.
  • Forgetting the inverted image when drawing the telescope's light path.

  1. Press "Telescope".
  2. Leave the lenses at fO = 200 cm and fE = 20 cm with a relaxed eye.
  3. Read both angles: the light entering your naked eye, and the exit beam.
  4. Divide the exit angle by the naked-eye angle: that is the magnification.
