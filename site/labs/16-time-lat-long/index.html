Labs 16-17 · Time, Latitude, Longitude

Every place on Earth has an address in degrees, and every address has a clock. Both come from the same grid, and one number, 15 degrees per hour, links them.

Words in this lab

  • graticule — the grid of latitude and longitude lines wrapped around the Earth.
  • equator — the big circle halfway between the poles. Latitude 0.
  • latitude — degrees north or south of the equator.
  • prime meridian — the north-south line through Greenwich, England. Longitude 0.
  • longitude — degrees east or west of the prime meridian.
  • meridian — the north-south line that runs over your head.
  • UTC — the clock of the Greenwich zone: the world's zero time zone.
  • International Date Line — the line near longitude 180 where a new calendar day starts.

The grid wrapped around the Earth is called the graticule. Two reference lines define it. The equator is the big circle halfway between the poles, latitude 0. Latitude counts degrees north (N) or south (S) of it, up to 90 at the poles. The prime meridian runs through Greenwich, east of London, longitude 0. Longitude counts degrees east (E) or west (W) of it, up to 180. A place is named by both, with the direction letter attached. New York, for example, sits at latitude 41 degrees north, longitude 74 degrees west. Drop the direction and the same numbers name a different place. So the letter is part of the answer, not decoration.

Why angles are clocks

Earth turns 360 deg in 24 hours. So it swings 15 deg every hour. That one ratio is the whole of timekeeping geography. One hour of clock equals 15 deg of longitude. One degree of longitude equals 4 minutes of solar time. When the Sun stands on your meridian, it is noon for everyone on that meridian. Your meridian is the north-south line that runs over your head. Someone 30 deg east saw noon two hours ago.

In 1884, the world agreed to slice the sphere into 24 standard zones. Each zone is 15 deg wide, centered on a zone meridian. The zone holding Greenwich is the zero point, called UTC. Each zone's clock reads one hour per zone away from Greenwich: east is ahead, west is behind. Real zone edges bend around borders and clocks, and daylight saving shifts them again. But this sim uses the ideal standard zones only. So hand-computing with degrees always agrees with the sim.

The date line

Clocks can say the hour, but not the day. So somebody has to declare where a new day starts. That line runs near the 180th meridian, in the quietest place on the planet: the International Date Line. Crossing it westward adds a day to your calendar. Crossing it eastward subtracts one. Travel west around the world. Unless you do this, your shore-side diary ends up one day ahead of your shipboard log. Two things change a date: passing midnight, and crossing the line. Keep both accountings separate. Then the problems solve themselves.

Why this matters to astronomers: longitude is time. An observability window promised in "9 PM" is meaningless until you know the longitude. Telescopes in Chile, the Canary Islands, and Hawaii hand over the sky to each other on exactly this arithmetic. Latitude sets what you can see. The altitude of the celestial pole above your horizon equals your latitude. Whether a star never sets depends on it too. The Sun's noon height through the year is also a latitude signature. The celestial sphere simply copies the graticule into the sky as RA and Dec (Lab 3). (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 1 and Ch. 2, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Swapping the sign and the direction letter: writing "74 deg E" for New York because the math produced -74. Check which way the place actually lies.
  • Forgetting the day change at the date line. The clock can land on 2 AM while the day has already flipped.
  • Converting with the city's actual clock (DST, political zones) when the question asks for the degree math. Use the ideal 15-deg zones here.
  • Dividing by 15 and dropping the remainder. 112.5 deg is 7.5 zones, and half a zone of time is 30 minutes, not 50.
  • Applying the day change twice: once for midnight, once for the line. The road you took may have crossed only one of them.
