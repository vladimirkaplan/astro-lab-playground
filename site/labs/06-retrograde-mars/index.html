Lab 6 · Retrograde motion of Mars

Twice a decade Mars stops, backs up across the stars for a couple of months, stops again, and goes forward. Nothing changed out there. Earth, on the faster inner lane, overtook it. This lab makes that pass visible from both ends: the top-down race track where the overtake happens, and the night sky where it looks like a backward loop.

What this lab is about

Mars is a slow walker against the stars. Night after night it creeps eastward along the ecliptic, the same direction the Sun and Moon travel. Then, near the middle of its brightest months, the crawl halts, the planet reverses and walks westward for weeks, halts a second time, and resumes its eastward march. That backward stretch is retrograde motion, and on the sky it draws a loose loop or an S. Mars is not doing anything strange. You are. Earth rides a smaller, faster orbit, and roughly every two years it catches up to and passes Mars on the inside. During the pass, the line of sight from Earth to Mars swings around, and a line of sight that swings swings the direction it points. That is the whole story.

Overtaking on the inside

Picture the two orbits as lanes on a track, Sun at the middle. Earth's lane is tighter, so Earth laps the sky at about 0.99 degrees a day; Mars, farther out, manages only about 0.52 degrees a day. When Earth is behind Mars and then swings past it, imagine holding a laser from Earth aimed at Mars and letting the beam land on the distant wall of stars. As long as Earth trails or leads cleanly, the dot drifts steadily one way. But in the brief moment Earth draws level with and passes Mars, the beam has to sweep backward across the wall to keep tracking the planet - exactly the way roadside signs seem to slide backward past your car window as you overtake them, even though the signs are bolted to the ground. The apparent backward slide is a parallax of motion, produced by your own faster orbit, and it can only happen to a planet farther from the Sun than you are. That single fact is why the retrograde planets are always the superior ones, Mars included.

What "station" means

The planet is not instantly moving west. It slows, momentarily travels neither east nor west, then accelerates the other way. Those two turn-around instants - where the apparent speed crosses zero and the sign flips - are the stations. A planet stands still against the stars at a station, which is literally where the old word comes from (Latin statio, a standing still). The first station opens the retrograde, the second closes it, and opposition - the moment Mars sits directly opposite the Sun, rising as the Sun sets, full and closest - falls between them, very near the middle of the loop. Retrograde and opposition are the same event seen from two angles: you can only overtake a planet when you come up on it from behind the Sun, so Mars is always retrograde when it is opposite the Sun, and always opposite the Sun when it is retrograde.

The epicycle: a beautiful fix that saved the wrong picture

Ancient sky-watchers had no idea Earth moved, and the honest reading of the evidence was that it does not - you cannot feel a 30 km/s orbital ride. To keep Earth fixed and still reproduce the loops, the Ptolemaic system gave each planet a second wheel: the planet rode a small circle, the epicycle, whose center in turn rode a big circle, the deferent, around the Earth. When the planet's motion on the epicycle pointed it backward, that backward bit was the retrograde - so the machinery was not a guess, it was tuned to reproduce the loop you will draw in this lab, with the stations at the points where the epicycle's motion cancels the deferent's. The price was two circles per planet instead of one, and a built-in coincidence: the epicycle period had to track the Sun (a planet goes retrograde only at opposition), which in the real Solar System is simply a consequence of Earth's own year. This is the same move you saw in the Venus lab, where the epicycle was bolted to the Earth-Sun line; there it predicted Venus could never show a full phase, and Galileo's telescope killed it. Mars's epicycle had no such clean phase test, so it survived far longer - it could save the observations, it just could not save the elegance.

How this lab runs

Bench 1 is the race track: a top-down, Sun-centered view of Earth and Mars on circular orbits. A sightline runs from Earth through Mars out to a ring of fixed stars, and the mark on that ring is where you would plot Mars that night. Scrub the date or press play and watch the ring mark glide forward, stall at a station, carve the loop backward, and recover - Earth and Mars highlighted when they line up at opposition. Flip to the sky view and the very same dates redraw that history the way you would actually see it: Mars tracing the retrograde hook against a fixed-star grid, with both stations and the opposition marked. Why this matters to astronomers: retrograde loops were the original data any world-system had to explain, and the argument turned on it well before telescopes. Copernicus placed Earth among the planets precisely so the loops fell out as a viewpoint effect with no epicycles required for them; the geometry in Bench 1 is his argument in motion. The same apparent-motion logic still governs when a spacecraft can be tracked, when a planet is recoverable in a survey, and why "apparent retrograde" is a routine line in every ephemeris - it is the Sun-Earth-observer catching up to the target, never the target truly reversing.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Thinking Mars actually slows down or reverses. Its heliocentric motion never changes sign here; only the apparent, Earth-seen direction does.
  • Expecting retrograde at random times. For a superior planet it happens only near opposition, and it is centered on opposition - the loop straddles it.
  • Confusing the two "stops." Motion goes E, then west, then E; the two stations are the two sign changes. Opposition is neither - it is the middle, when Mars moves fastest westward, not when it stops.
  • Giving the epicycle credit for being wrong everywhere. It reproduced the loop on purpose; it failed on elegance and, for Venus and Mercury, on phases.
  • Calling Earth's shadow or Mars's tilt the cause. This is pure geometry of relative motion - no shadow and no season is involved.
  • Marking a station wherever Mars is brightest. That is opposition; the station is where the apparent speed hits zero, which is earlier and later on either side.

Both benches compute Mars's apparent motion from a single idealized model in mars.js: Earth and Mars on fixed, flat Keplerian ellipses frozen at the J2000.0 epoch (orbital elements from NASA planetary fact sheets), with eccentricity kept because ignoring it drifts the apparition by about two weeks. It is a teaching model, not an almanac: the window's stations land within about a week and a half of published dates and opposition within a day or two. Bench 1 draws the orbits as circles for the viewpoint argument and marks both stations and the opposition. The sky view plots apparent longitude against date on a fixed-star grid - because the model is coplanar, Mars stays on the ecliptic, so the loop opens as a backward bend rather than a two-dimensional curl (a real Mars also dips a little in latitude).
