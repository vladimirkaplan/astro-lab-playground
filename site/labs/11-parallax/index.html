Lab 11 · Heliocentric parallax

Earth travels 300 million kilometres between January and July. That is the longest yardstick in astronomy. Nearby stars cannot hide from it: they rock back and forth against the deep background once a year. Measure the rock, and you have the star's distance by pure geometry - no physics, no calibration, no assumptions about what the star is made of.

Words in this lab

  • parallax — a near thing seems to shift against far things when you move.
  • baseline — the known length you measure an angle against.
  • arcsecond — 1/3600 of a degree. A very small angle.
  • milliarcsecond (mas) — 1/1000 of an arcsecond.
  • ecliptic — the line of the Sun's yearly path across the stars. Earth's orbit lies in this plane.
  • light-year — the distance light travels in one year.
  • parsec — a distance unit: about 3.26 light-years.

What this lab is about

Hold your thumb at arm's length and close one eye, then the other. The thumb jumps against the wall. Your eyes are 6 cm apart. Earth's orbit is 300 million km across. A star seen from opposite ends of that diameter shifts by the same trick. The shift of a nearby star against far-off background stars over six months is annual parallax. It is the only distance measurement in astronomy that is pure trigonometry. Half of the full January-to-July swing is called the parallax p. We measure it in arcseconds, or more usefully in milliarcseconds (mas).

The parsec, and why half the swing

Astronomers built the unit backwards from the measurement, so the formula is a division. With p in arcseconds, the distance is d = 1/p parsecs. With p in milliarcseconds, d = 1000 / p. One parsec is 3.26156 light years, a NIST-rounded conversion. At that distance, the radius of Earth's orbit (1 AU) shows a shift of 1 arcsec. One AU is the Earth-Sun distance, about 150 million km. Why is half the swing the answer? The triangle uses the Earth-Sun distance (1 AU), not the full January-to-July baseline (2 AU). The full annual E-W sweep of a star is twice the parallax. The parallax is half that sweep. Confusing the two multiplies or divides every distance you publish by 2. History has no shortage of people who did just that.

The shape of the wobble: an ellipse, usually squashed

A star does not rock along a line. It walks a small ellipse in a year. The long half-axis is always p, along ecliptic longitude. The short half-axis is p × sin(β). The angle β is the star's ecliptic latitude. That is its angle above or below the plane of Earth's orbit. This is the trap in this lab, so say it out loud before touching the sim:

  • β = 90° (star at the ecliptic pole): sin(β) = 1, so the ellipse is a circle of radius p.
  • β = 0° (star on the ecliptic): sin(β) = 0. The short half-axis is zero. The "ellipse" collapses to a line whose half-length is p. The star just rocks back and forth.
  • In between: an ellipse squashed by exactly sin(β). Barnard's Star at β = +47° keeps 73% of its circle. Wolf 359 at β = -8° keeps 14% and traces a razor-thin sliver.

The direction of travel also flips with the sign of β. Stars north of the ecliptic and stars south of it go around their ellipses in opposite senses. Drag the latitude slider in Bench 1 until the ellipse degenerates. After that you will never mix up sin(β) and cos(β) in this context again.

Why the Greeks found nothing

The failure of stellar parallax was the strongest objection to a moving Earth for two thousand years. And it was a reasonable objection. If Earth races around the Sun, the stars must visibly shift. They do not. The numbers now show why nobody could see it. The parallax of the nearest star systems is at most about 768 mas. Proxima Centauri's 768 milliarcseconds is 0.77 arcsec. A trained naked eye can judge a position to about 1 arcminute. This shift is roughly one two-hundredth of that, and the next-nearest stars are smaller still. With no instrument finer than the eye, the only honest conclusion was "no shift detected". And the choices on the table were these: Earth stands still, or the stars are unimaginably far away. Aristarchus had already argued the second. Nobody could prove it until the telescope and the micrometer existed. Friedrich Bessel finally measured the 310 mas parallax of 61 Cygni in 1838. The two-millennium argument ended in one publication.

The first rung of the distance ladder

Parallax is the first rung of the cosmic distance ladder. It stands on nothing else: a baseline of 2 AU, an angle, and trigonometry. Every brighter tool is an indirect method. Cepheid variables, the tip of the red giant branch, and Type Ia supernovae all must be calibrated against something. That something is parallaxes of stars inside our own galaxy. Get parallax wrong and every distance in the universe moves with it. That is why space missions like Hipparcos and Gaia exist. Their job is to measure angles smaller than a mas across the whole sky.

How this lab runs

Bench 1 animates a year of wobble for any of the eight nearest star systems. Sliders for parallax and ecliptic latitude let you watch the ellipse squash flat. Bench 2 hands you a hidden target star and a reticle: a fine crosshair for measuring positions. Take the January and July positions and halve the shift. Then divide 1000 by the result. You have done what Bessel did. The page grades your distance against the catalog value for the star you drew. Ideal Mode is exact. Fun Mode adds a few mas of instrument jitter to every reading. That jitter is roughly what makes real parallax work hard. (Course framing follows OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 2, CC BY 4.0.)

Why this matters to astronomers: parallax anchors the entire distance scale of the universe. Gaia's parallaxes rewired galactic structure. The Hubble tension is a live dispute about the universe's expansion rate. At its heart it asks whether the first rungs of the ladder are calibrated correctly. Every number in this lab is the number a professional actually measures - just in bigger units.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Using the full January-to-July shift as the parallax. That shift spans a 2 AU baseline, so it is 2p; the parallax is half of it.
  • Flipping the ellipse rule. sin(0°) = 0, so a star ON the ecliptic traces a line. A star at the ecliptic POLE traces a circle. If your sketch says the opposite, you used cos.
  • Mixing milliarcseconds and arcseconds in d = 1/p. With mas the formula is d = 1000/p; a forgotten 1000 turns Proxima into 1300 pc.
  • Saying the Greeks "missed" parallax by bad luck. 768 mas is tens to hundreds of times below what a naked eye can use for positions. Detection was physically impossible before precision instruments.
  • Thinking parallax measures brightness or size. It measures an angle against a known baseline; the star's nature is irrelevant to the distance.
  • Assuming a bigger parallax means a farther star. The values p and d are inverses: the biggest wobble belongs to the closest star.

Measurement log

Parallaxes, magnitudes, and spectral types are rounded public catalog values (Gaia EDR3 where that is the commonly quoted figure, Hipparcos for Tau Ceti); ecliptic latitudes are rounded to whole degrees and are good enough to draw an honest ellipse shape, not a star position. Both benches idealise Earth's orbit as circular and use the small-angle approximation, which is excellent at these angles. Bench 2's two nights are idealised as the nights when the target sits at its extreme east and west positions, six months apart - that is the geometry in which the measured shift is exactly 2p. Fun Mode's 1-2 mas jitter is a teaching illustration of instrument noise, not a photometric error model. No data about any student is stored or sent anywhere; your log lives in this browser only.
