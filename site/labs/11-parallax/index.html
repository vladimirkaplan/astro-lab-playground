Lab 11 · Heliocentric parallax

Earth travels 300 million kilometres between January and July. That is the longest yardstick in astronomy, and nearby stars cannot hide from it: they rock back and forth against the deep background once a year. Measure the rock, and you have the star's distance by pure geometry - no physics, no calibration, no assumptions about what the star is made of.

What this lab is about

Hold a thumb at arm's length and close one eye, then the other: the thumb jumps against the wall. The eyes are 6 cm apart; Earth's orbit is 300 million km across, and a star seen from opposite ends of that diameter shifts by the same trick. The shift of a nearby star against far-off background stars over six months is annual parallax, and it is the only distance measurement in astronomy that is pure trigonometry. Half of the full January to July swing is defined as the parallax p, measured in arcseconds or, more usefully, milliarcseconds (mas).

The parsec, and why half the swing

Astronomers built the unit backwards from the measurement, so the formula is a division: with p in arcseconds, distance d = 1/p parsecs; with p in milliarcseconds, d = 1000 / p. One parsec (3.26156 light years, a NIST-rounded conversion) is exactly the distance at which a star parallaxes by 1 arcsec across a 1 AU radius of orbit. Half the swing is the answer because the triangle uses the Earth-Sun distance (1 AU), not the full January-to-July baseline (2 AU): a star's full annual E-W sweep is 2p, and the parallax is p. Confusing the two multiplies or divides every distance you publish by 2, and history contains no shortage of people who did.

The shape of the wobble: an ellipse, usually squashed

A star does not rock along a line - it walks a small ellipse in a year. The semi-major axis is always p, along ecliptic longitude. The semi-minor axis is p × sin(β), where β is the star's ecliptic latitude - its angle above or below the plane of Earth's orbit. This is the trap in this lab, so say it out loud before touching the sim:

  • β = 90° (star at the ecliptic pole): sin(β) = 1, the ellipse is a circle of radius p.
  • β = 0° (star on the ecliptic): sin(β) = 0, the semi-minor axis is zero and the "ellipse" collapses to a line segment of half-length p. The star just rocks back and forth.
  • In between: an ellipse squashed by exactly sin(β). Barnard's Star at β = +47° keeps 73% of its circle; Wolf 359 at β = -8° keeps 14% and traces a razor-thin sliver.

The direction of travel also flips with the sign of β: stars north of the ecliptic and stars south of it go around their ellipses in opposite senses. Drag the latitude slider in Bench 1 until the ellipse degenerates, and you will never mix up sin(β) and cos(β) in this context again.

Why the Greeks found nothing

The failure of stellar parallax was the strongest objection to a moving Earth for two thousand years, and it was a reasonable objection: if Earth races around the Sun, the stars must visibly shift, and they do not. The numbers now show why they could not see it. The parallax of the nearest star systems is at most about 768 mas - Proxima Centauri's 768 milliarcseconds is 0.77 arcsec, roughly one two-hundredth of the ~1 arcminute angular resolution a trained naked eye can judge position against, and the next-nearest stars are smaller still. With no instrument finer than the eye, the only honest conclusions were "no shift detected" - and the choices on the table were that Earth stands still, or that the stars are unimaginably far away. Aristarchus had already argued the second; nobody could prove it until the telescope and the micrometer existed. Friedrich Bessel finally measured the 310 mas parallax of 61 Cygni in 1838 and the two-millennium argument ended in one publication.

The first rung of the distance ladder

Parallax is called the first rung of the cosmic distance ladder because it stands on nothing else: a baseline of 2 AU, an angle, and trigonometry. Every brighter tool - Cepheid variables, the tip of the red giant branch, Type Ia supernovae - is an indirect method that must be calibrated against something, and that something is parallaxes of stars inside our own galaxy. Get parallax wrong and every distance in the universe moves with it, which is why space missions like Hipparcos and Gaia exist purely to measure angles smaller than a mas across the whole sky.

Why this matters to astronomers: parallax anchors the entire distance scale of the universe. Gaia's parallaxes rewired galactic structure, and any tension in the expansion rate of the universe (the Hubble tension) is ultimately a story about whether the first rungs of the ladder are calibrated correctly. Every number in this lab is the number a professional actually measures - just in bigger units.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Using the full January-to-July shift as p. The shift across a 2 AU baseline is 2p; the parallax is half of it.
  • Flipping the ellipse rule. sin(0°) = 0, so a star ON the ecliptic traces a line, and a star at the ecliptic POLE traces a circle. If your sketch says the opposite, you used cos.
  • Mixing milliarcseconds and arcseconds in d = 1/p. With mas the formula is d = 1000/p; a forgotten 1000 turns Proxima into 1300 pc.
  • Saying the Greeks "missed" parallax by bad luck. 768 mas is tens to hundreds of times below naked-eye positional resolution; detection was physically impossible before precision instruments.
  • Thinking parallax measures brightness or size. It measures an angle against a known baseline; the star's nature is irrelevant to the distance.
  • Assuming a bigger parallax means a farther star. p and d are inverses: the biggest wobble belongs to the closest star.

Measurement log

Parallaxes, magnitudes, and spectral types are rounded public catalog values (Gaia EDR3 where that is the commonly quoted figure, Hipparcos for Tau Ceti); ecliptic latitudes are rounded to whole degrees and are good enough to draw an honest ellipse shape, not a star position. Both benches idealise Earth's orbit as circular and use the small-angle approximation, which is excellent at these angles. Bench 2's two nights are idealised as the nights when the target sits at its extreme east and west positions, six months apart - that is the geometry in which the measured shift is exactly 2p. Fun Mode's 1-2 mas jitter is a teaching illustration of instrument noise, not a photometric error model. No data about any student is stored or sent anywhere; your log lives in this browser only.
