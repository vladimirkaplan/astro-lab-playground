Mathematics for astronomy

Three tools that show up in every later lab: pi as a measured ratio, the small-angle formula that turns arcseconds into kilometers, and scientific notation that keeps big numbers safe in your arithmetic.

Words in this lab

  • ratio — one value divided by another value.
  • diameter — the distance straight across a circle, through the middle.
  • pi — the ratio of a circle's distance around to its diameter. About 3.14.
  • percent error — how far your value is from the accepted value, as a percent.
  • angular diameter — how wide an object looks in the sky.
  • arcsecond — 1/3600 of a degree. A very small angle.
  • scientific notation — writing big numbers as one digit times a power of ten.

What this lab is about

Astronomy is a measurement science with a scale problem. Its objects are too round, too far, or too big to touch. So this whole course leans on three small pieces of math. This lab rehearses all three on one bench. You will measure pi. You will trade angle for size with the small-angle formula. And you will read big numbers without flinching.

Pi is a ratio you measure, not a digit string you recite

Wrap a string around any circle. Cut it, lay it flat, and divide its length by the diameter. That number is pi, and it comes out the same for a coin and for the Earth's orbit. Pi is a ratio: one length divided by another. The number 3.14 is the value this course treats as given. Real pi has more digits than you will ever need. This lab is not about getting 3.14159 out of a ruler. It never comes out of a ruler. It is about measuring as carefully as you can, then computing percent error against the accepted value. A 1 percent result from string and ruler is a good measurement, not a failed one.

The small-angle formula: trading angle for size

Most astronomy objects are very far away. The triangle from your eye to their two edges is then thin enough to flatten. Angular diameter, true size, and distance trade against each other in one line:

delta (arcsec) = 206,265 x d / D

Here d and D are in the same units. The angular diameter, delta, is how wide the object looks in the sky. The big constant is not magic. It is the number of arcseconds packed into one radian (180/pi x 3600 = 206,264.8, course-rounded). An object that sits one diameter away from you subtends one radian. A radian is 206,265 arcseconds. That is the whole trick. Know any two of the three quantities and the formula hands you the third. That is how a first-week student can size the Sun.

The eclipse coincidence

Set the sim to the Moon, then to the Sun. The telescope inset barely changes. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon. It is also about 400 times farther away. So their angular sizes land within a few percent of each other. Total solar eclipses exist because of that accident. Ring-shaped annular eclipses exist because the Moon's distance wobbles a little. Nobody arranged it.

Scientific notation is a seatbelt, not a dialect

384,400 km is a number with a stray pair of zeros you might drop. 3.84 x 10^5 km says "a few hundred thousand" out loud and survives being copied. That second style is scientific notation. Move the decimal until one digit is left of it. The exponent counts the places you moved. Every multiplication becomes adding exponents, which is why astronomers think in powers of ten. Write 1.5 x 10^8, not 1.5E8, in a lab report.

Why this matters to astronomers: the small-angle formula is the first step of almost every distance method in this course. It is still professional equipment. Interferometers measure a star's angular width. With a known distance, this formula gives the star's true width. And the same three-variable trade goes back to Aristarchus sizing the Moon's orbit from a half-moon angle. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 1, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Mixing units in the small-angle formula. d and D must be in the SAME unit. The ratio is what the 206,265 turns into arcseconds.
  • Entering 206,265 as a measured value. It is a unit conversion baked into the formula, like 100 cm per meter.
  • Reporting pi = 3.1 from a string and calling it exact. Or reporting 3.14159 from the same string and calling it a measurement. Report what you measured, then the percent error.
  • Blaming the Moon's shape for its angular size. Distance, not shape, is what changes. The Moon looks 14 percent wider across the sky at perigee, its closest point, because it is 14 percent nearer.
  • Writing 384 x 10^3 for 384,400. Legal arithmetic, but the notation means one digit left of the decimal: 3.844 x 10^5.
