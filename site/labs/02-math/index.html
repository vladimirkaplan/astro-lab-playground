Mathematics for astronomy

Three tools that show up in every later lab: pi as a measured ratio, the small-angle formula that turns arcseconds into kilometers, and scientific notation that keeps astronomese out of your arithmetic.

What this lab is about

Astronomy is a measurement science with a scale problem. The things it measures are either too round, too far, or too big to touch, so the whole course leans on three small pieces of mathematics. This lab rehearses all three on one bench: measure pi, trade angle for size with the small-angle formula, and read big numbers without flinching.

Pi is a ratio you measure, not a digit string you recite

Wrap a string around any circle, cut it, lay it flat, divide its length by the diameter. That number is pi, and it comes out the same for a coin and for the Earth's orbit. The number 3.14 is the value this course treats as given; the real pi has more digits than you will ever need. The lab is not about getting 3.14159 out of a ruler - it never comes out of a ruler. It is about measuring as carefully as you can, computing percent error against the accepted value, and knowing that a 1 percent result from string and ruler is a good measurement, not a failed one.

The small-angle formula: trading angle for size

Most astronomy objects are so far away that the triangle from your eye to their two edges is thin enough to flatten. Then angular diameter, true size and distance trade against each other in one line:

delta (arcsec) = 206,265 x d / D

where d and D are in the same units. The big constant is not magic: it is the number of arcseconds packed into one radian (180/pi x 3600 = 206,264.8, course-rounded). Anything that sits one diameter away from you subtends one radian; a radian is 206,265 arcseconds; that is the whole trick. Know any two of the three quantities and the formula hands you the third, which is how a first-week student can weigh the Sun.

The eclipse coincidence

Hold the sim on the Moon, then on the Sun, and the telescope inset barely changes. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and also about 400 times farther away, so their angular sizes land within a few percent of each other. Total solar eclipses exist because of that accident, and ring-shaped annular ones exist because the Moon's distance wobbles a little. Nobody arranged it.

Scientific notation is a seatbelt, not a dialect

384,400 km is a number with a stray pair of zeros you might drop; 3.84 x 10^5 km says "a few hundred thousand" out loud and survives being copied. Move the decimal to leave one digit left of it; the exponent counts the places you moved. Every multiplication becomes adding exponents, which is why astronomers think in powers of ten. Write 1.5 x 10^8, not 1.5E8, in a lab report.

Why this matters to astronomers: the small-angle formula is the first rung of nearly every distance ladder rung in this course, and it is still professional equipment: angular diameters measured by interferometers plus a known distance give a star's true width, and the same three-variable trade dates back to Aristarchus sizing the Moon's orbit from a half-moon angle. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Mixing units in the small-angle formula. d and D must be in the SAME unit; the ratio is what the 206,265 turns into arcseconds.
  • Entering 206,265 as a measured value. It is a unit conversion baked into the formula, like 100 cm per meter.
  • Reporting pi = 3.1 from a string and calling it exact, or 3.14159 from the same string and calling it a measurement. Report what you measured, then the percent error.
  • Blaming the Moon's shape for its angular size. Distance, not shape, is what changes: the Moon is 14 percent wider across the sky at perigee than apogee because it is 14 percent nearer.
  • Writing 384 x 10^3 for 384,400. Legal arithmetic, but notation means one digit left of the decimal: 3.844 x 10^5.
