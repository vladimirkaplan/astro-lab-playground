Lab 8 · Acceleration Due to Gravity

A string, a bob, a stopwatch, and the most famous number in the introductory lab: 9.8 m/s², measured with your own thumb on a timer.

What this lab is about

You hang a bob on a string, pull it aside a little, let it swing, and time it. The course runs fixed string lengths - 30, 55, 75, and 100 cm - and times fifteen complete swings each time, then plots period-squared against length. The points fall on a straight line, and the steepness of that line contains g, the acceleration due to gravity. One stopwatch, one string, and you can weigh the Earth.

The physics

Gravity pulls the bob toward its resting spot with a force proportional to sin(angle). For small swings, sin(angle) and angle are almost the same thing, and the equation of motion becomes the classic one whose solution is a pure sine wave - simple harmonic motion. Its period is T = 2*pi*sqrt(L/g). Three things fall out of that formula, and all three show up in the lab questions:

  • Length matters, as a square root. Four times the string only doubles the period. Plot T-squared against L and the square root disappears: T² = (4*pi²/g) L, a line through the origin with slope 4*pi²/g.
  • Mass does not matter. Heavier means more force but also more inertia; the two cancel exactly, the same cancellation that makes feathers and hammers fall together on the Moon.
  • Amplitude barely matters - for small angles. The exact period grows by about 0.3% at 12 degrees and 1.7% at 30. That tiny correction is the real subject of the phrase "small-angle approximation."

Why fifteen swings instead of one? Your reaction time (call it a quarter of a second) divides by fifteen. Timing fifteen swings spreads human error the way averaging does, which is the general lesson the Check tab quizzes.

Why this matters to astronomers: g is the first value any planet gets, and every gravity measurement in astronomy - weighing stars in binary systems, black holes at galaxy cores, the density of the universe itself - is the same trick scaled up: orbital period and distance in, mass out. Galileo's pendulum thinking leads straight to Kepler's third law, which leads to weighing everything. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 2 and Ch. 5, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Using pi = 3 during the math when the course says 3.14; the third digit matters at this precision.
  • Plotting T against L and then wondering why the "line" curves; the lab wants T².
  • Timing one swing and trusting a thumb that moves in tenths of a second.
  • Counting "one" at the start - you want to count crossings, and let the pendulum settle before gate one.
  • Measuring L from the pivot to where the string is tied instead of to the bob's center.
  • Forgetting the percent error against 9.8 in the write-up.
