Lab 1 · Measurement

Rulers, balances, two unit systems, and the honest question behind every measurement: how far is your number from the accepted one?

What this lab is about

The first lab of the course is not really about the table or the coins. It is about how to measure anything and report it honestly. You measure one object twice, once with a meter stick in centimeters and once with a yard stick in inches, then divide the two answers to discover the centimeters-per-inch conversion factor from your own hands instead of a formula sheet. Then you weigh coins, convert between unit systems, and learn one reusable habit: compare every result to the accepted value with percent error.

The physics and the metrology

A ruler can only be read to its finest tick plus one estimated digit. A meter stick has millimeter ticks, so a careful reading goes to the nearest tenth of a centimeter, not further. Two effects eat precision before any math starts: parallax, where viewing the scale from an angle projects the object edge onto the wrong scale mark (the error grows with the gap between object and scale), and alignment, where you measure from an end of the ruler instead of the zero mark, or forget to subtract the starting mark.

An instrument reading is never the last word either. Repeated readings scatter (random error), and a badly zeroed instrument shifts every reading the same way (systematic error). Percent error, |measured minus accepted| / accepted times 100, summarizes accuracy as one number, and it stays unsigned so an early high reading and a late low one score the same.

Mass and weight are different quantities. Mass counts the matter in an object and is the same everywhere; weight is the gravitational force on that matter and changes with location. A balance compares the weight of the object to the weight of standard masses, so it reads mass correctly anywhere, even on the Moon.

Why this matters to astronomers: almost nothing in astronomy can be measured directly. Every distance, mass, temperature, or abundance in the field is an inference from small numbers that each carry their own percent error, which then multiply through the calculation. Conversion discipline is not busywork: a crashed Mars orbiter in 1999 is the famous price of mixing English and metric quantities. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 1-2, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Measuring from the physical end of a ruler instead of the zero mark.
  • Reading the scale from the side instead of straight above (parallax).
  • Recording digits finer than the instrument resolves, or rounding before the final step.
  • Flipping the conversion factor upside down and getting 0.394 cm per inch.
  • Writing the accepted value into the data table as if it were your reading.
  • Reporting a signed percent error when the course convention is magnitude.
