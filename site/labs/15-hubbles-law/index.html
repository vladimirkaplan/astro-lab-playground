Lab 15 · Hubble's Law

Dots on a balloon become galaxies in an expanding universe. Measure, plot, fit a slope, and read the age of the balloon cosmos off that slope.

What this lab is about

You draw ten spots on a balloon: one is home (the Milky Way), the rest are galaxies. You measure the string distance from home to every galaxy, inflate the balloon, measure again, and divide the change by the seconds inflation took. Now every galaxy has a distance and a recession speed. Plot them and the points line up: farther galaxies recede faster, in proportion. That proportionality is Hubble's law, and its slope is the Hubble constant.

The physics: Velocity = H times Distance

When space itself stretches, everything unbound inside it drifts apart at a rate set by how much new space appears in between. Double the distance and you double the intervening stretching, so speed scales with distance: V = H · D. One consequence students miss: no place is special. An observer on any balloon dot sees the same law, because uniform expansion looks the same from every point. There is no center of the surface, and the balloon surface has no edge.

Inflate from radius factor 1 to factor a over time t and the arithmetic works out to H = (a - 1) / (a · t) when velocities are measured against the final distances. The lab then treats 1/H as the age of the balloon universe: how long back you must unwind the expansion before all distances shrink to zero.

For the real sky, Hubble's constant sits near 70 km/s per megaparsec. The conversion to years is one neat line: 1/H ≈ 977.8 / H0 gigayears, so H = 70 gives roughly 14 billion years, within a rounding error of the measured 13.8. Modern teams disagree at the 5-to-10-percent level between early-universe and late-universe methods (the Hubble tension), which is exactly the kind of live disagreement that makes a constant famous.

Why this matters to astronomers: v = H·D turns velocity into a distance ladder rung you can reach for any faint galaxy with a spectrum: measure the redshift (Lab 9 gave you the lines to measure it on), divide by H, done. And 1/H gives the age of the universe with one assumption - that expansion ran at a steady pace - which is close enough to be meaningful and wrong enough to have launched a Nobel Prize when they found the expansion accelerating. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Letting the best-fit line find its own intercept - expansion says zero distance means zero speed, so pin the line through the origin.
  • Measuring chord through the balloon instead of string along the skin; the analogy lives on the surface.
  • Plotting velocity against the first distances; the law pairs v with the distances measured with that velocity.
  • Reporting the slope as an age without flipping it: age is 1 over the slope, not the slope.
  • Carrying balloon units (cm/s) into cosmic units (km/s/Mpc) without converting anything.
  • Thinking the dots slide across the surface. On the real sky, the unbound galaxies are carried apart by growth in space itself.
