Lab 14 · Galaxy identification

Ten real galaxies from Wikimedia Commons. Learn the tuning fork, then sort each image by family and stage, and finally name galaxies you have met - the way the real lab asks you to work, from pictures instead of notes.

What this lab is about

Hubble sorted galaxies by picture, and the sort still carries his shape of thought: a tuning fork. The handle is the ellipticals, E0 to E7 - smooth, round-to-oval glows of old stars with no arms and barely any gas. The two prongs are the spirals: ordinary ones (S) on one prong, barred ones (SB) on the other. Each prong runs a, b, c: an a spiral has a big bright bulge and arms wound so tight they can barely be counted; a c spiral is mostly arms - loose, patchy, rich in gas and newborn stars - around a small bulge; b is in between. S0 (or SA0/SB0) sits at the fork point: a disc with a bulge but no arms at all. Off to the side are the irregulars and peculiars: galaxies with no orderly shape to grade, many of them because a companion recently shook them.

Reading an edge-on disc

A spiral seen face-on is obvious. Seen edge-on it becomes a lens: a bright elongated middle (the bulge, viewed from its side) tapering to thin points, and very often a dark straight dust lane slicing across the light - the whole disc’s dust concentrated into one line along the middle of the disc. Lens shape plus dust lane means “disc, seen sideways”, not “blob”. The two edge-on galaxies in this set, M104 and NGC 253, are ordinary spirals; their strangeness is only orientation. If you can flip the same galaxy in your mind to face-on, you would count arms in both of them.

Why M87 is not “just blurry”

The first time students see a giant elliptical they reach for “out of focus”. But blur is an instrumental excuse, and ellipticals survive focus: even in the sharpest images M87 has no cold gas, no dust lane, no spiral arms, no star-forming knots - nothing but a smooth, twice-brightening glow and its old red stars. That emptiness is the type. A blurry spiral still shows dust and hints of arms once you stretch the image; an elliptical never does. (M87 does have one famous non-smooth feature - a jet from its central black hole - but a jet is an active nucleus, not a spiral arm.) Classification lives in the pattern of structure, or of honest structurelessness, not in color or sharpness alone: two galaxies can share a color and sit on opposite ends of the fork.

How astronomers actually classify

Morphology first: shape, symmetry, bulge-to-light ratio, bars, lanes, arms - what you are doing on the Simulate tab. Stage letters are judgement calls with fuzzy borders (a b/c object is graded honestly by picking b or c and defending it), and peculiars get tick marks and footnotes instead of gentle lies. Color and spectra come after, as cross-checks: blue means gas and young stars, so usually late-type or irregular; red-and-dead usually means early-type.

Why this matters to astronomers: the tuning fork is not a family tree - ellipticals did not grow into spirals - but every distance ladder, every survey and every galaxy-evolution story files its objects by this shape language first. Reading a galaxy’s type off an image is the oldest quantitative skill in extragalactic astronomy, and it starts exactly where this lab starts: decide disc-or-not, decide bar-or-not, decide tight-or-loose. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Calling an edge-on spiral “peculiar” because it looks odd - odd is just orientation; find the dust lane first.
  • Deciding elliptical-vs-spiral from colour alone; a smooth red blob and a smooth red elliptical are not the same call - look for disc, dust and arms.
  • Missing the bar because you looked for arms first; the bar is a straight lane of light through the core, and arms spring off its ends.
  • Reading a/b/c backwards: a = tight arms, big bulge; c = loose arms, small bulge.
  • Sorting M82 with “S, because it is elongated” - elongation alone is not a disc; ragged gas lanes with no pattern mean peculiar.
  • Forgetting that S0 exists, then forcing an armless disc into “a”.
