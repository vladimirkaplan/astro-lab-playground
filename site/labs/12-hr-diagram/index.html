Lab 12 · The H-R diagram

Two numbers per star: how hot, how bright. Plot those two against each other and stars refuse to scatter randomly. They line up, clump, and leave two empty corners. That arrangement is the most useful picture in astronomy. Every size you will read off it, you will calculate yourself.

Words in this lab

  • luminosity — the total power a star gives off as light.
  • radius — the distance from a star's center to its surface.
  • main sequence — the diagonal band where stars fuse hydrogen.
  • giant — a star that ran out of hydrogen in its core and swelled huge.
  • supergiant — an even bigger and brighter swollen star.
  • white dwarf — the exposed, cooling core of a dead star: Earth-size, Sun-mass.
  • decade — a factor of ten on a stretched (logarithmic) axis.
  • turnoff — the point where a cluster's heavy stars start leaving the main sequence.

What this lab is about

A temperature is easy to get from a spectrum. A luminosity takes one more ingredient, usually a distance. Those two numbers look like very little information about a star. But plot them against each other for a few dozen stars. The picture is not a cloud of dots. Most stars fall on a single diagonal band. A pocket of bright cool stars sits above it. A handful of faint hot stars stick in the bottom left. Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell found that arrangement independently around 1911-1913. This was before anyone knew stars shine by fusing hydrogen, and it organized the whole subject.

How to read the axes

  • Temperature runs backwards. Hot stars are on the left. The axis is also compressed. 40,000 K to 2,500 K share one row of pixels. The spacing between tick labels therefore gets wider as you go right. Astronomers are stuck with this convention, and so is this sim.
  • Luminosity is logarithmic. Each horizontal grid line is a factor of ten: one decade. The axes here span ten decades of power, and the stars in this lab cover five of them. That reaches from Wolf 359 at 0.0014 times the Sun to Deneb at about 200,000. Read a vertical gap in decades, never in centimetres.
  • Luminosity is the star, not the view. It is the power the star actually emits, in solar units. The brightness of a star as it reaches a telescope depends on distance too. That is why the lab needs distances before it can build a diagram.

The machine behind the picture

A star is a hot sphere. So the power leaving it is set by its surface area and by the fourth power of its surface temperature. In solar units that is L = R² (T / 5772)⁴. Two of those three quantities are what you measure. So the third comes out for free:

R = sqrt(L) / (T / 5772)²

This is the whole trick of the diagram. A cool star far above the main sequence is not a mystery. It is an enormous object. And a hot star in the bottom left has to be tiny. Temperature and luminosity are the measurement; radius is the verdict.

Main sequence: where stars spend their lives

The diagonal band is the main sequence, and it is where stars fuse hydrogen. Mass sets a star's position on it. That is why the band is a sequence at all. More mass means a hotter core and a hotter surface. Luminosity then climbs roughly as the 3.5 power of the mass. A 10 solar mass B star is a few thousand times more luminous than the Sun. It burns its fuel in a few tens of millions of years. A 0.1 solar mass M dwarf like Proxima Centauri puts out 0.0017 times the Sun. It will outlast the present age of the universe. The band has a thickness. Stars of the same colour are not all exactly alike, and real measurements carry error. Switch to Fun Mode in the sim and watch the band fatten.

Giants and supergiants: same colour, far more of it

Take Arcturus and the Sun. Arcturus is cooler, yet it shines about a hundred times brighter. Cooler means less power per square metre of surface. So the only way to win that argument is to have far more surface. The sim puts Arcturus near 19 solar radii. Giants are stars that have run out of hydrogen in their cores and swollen. Supergiants go further. Betelgeuse comes out near 930 solar radii, about 4.3 AU. That is past the orbit of Mars. Rigel is the opposite combination, and just as instructive. It is hot and 120,000 times the Sun. That works out near 79 solar radii: a blue supergiant.

White dwarfs: the bottom-left corner

Sirius B is 25,200 K, as hot as a B star, and puts out 0.026 times the Sun. Feed those into the radius formula. You get 0.0085 solar radii, about 5,900 km: the size of Earth, carrying the mass of a Sun. Nothing fuses down there. It is an exposed core cooling slowly. The bottom-left corner of the diagram is nearly empty. Matter has no business being both very hot and very dim. The exceptions: it is tiny, or it is heavily veiled.

What the diagram is not

It is not a map. Two stars drawn next to each other can be ten parsecs and ten thousand parsecs apart. The diagram plots properties, not positions. A star drifting right on the screen means its surface cooled, not that it moved. The diagram is also not a fixed portrait of any one star. As a star ages, it travels across the diagram. That is why astronomers read cluster ages off it. They look for the turnoff: the point where the most massive members have started leaving the main sequence.

Why this matters to astronomers: the H-R diagram turns a list of numbers into physics. It gives distances to star clusters and to other galaxies, and it dates them. It is also the scoreboard that the theory of stellar evolution has to match. The lab drills the habit that makes it work: measure two things, derive the third, and know which is which. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Reading the horizontal axis left to right as hotter. It runs the other way: a star to the right of the Sun is cooler than the Sun.
  • Measuring a vertical gap with a ruler. The luminosity axis is logarithmic, so equal distances are equal ratios.
  • Concluding red means small and dim. Betelgeuse is one of the coolest stars on the plot and one of the biggest objects in it.
  • Explaining a white dwarf with fusion. It is hot because it is a fresh, dense remnant radiating stored heat, not because it burns anything.
  • Forgetting the fourth power. Double the temperature at fixed size and you get 16 times the luminosity, not twice.
  • Quoting the sim radius as if it were measured. Interferometers do measure a few dozen stellar diameters directly. Everything here is derived from temperature and luminosity. That is what makes the number sensitive to bad data.
  • Treating the published luminosity class as gospel. Polaris is labelled class Ib, a supergiant, and still plots in the giant region of this diagram. Labels are conventions; positions are measurements.

Reading log

Each dot is one real star, plotted from rounded public catalog values of effective temperature and luminosity (Yale Bright Star Catalog and standard star fact sheets; the more-cited value was used where sources disagree). Radii are never typed in: the sim computes them from those two numbers with the Stefan-Boltzmann law, so a radius here can sit 10-30 percent away from a directly measured one, and that gap is rounding plus real variability in the star. The three white dwarfs ship a luminosity derived from their published temperature and radius, because printed white-dwarf luminosities for the same star differ by a factor of two between sources. Dot colours are approximate blackbody swatches, and Fun Mode noise is a teaching illustration, not a photometric error model. This is a properties plot, not a map of the sky, and it holds no data about any student.

Read the two numbers off the sim, then derive the third yourself. Ideal Mode gives exact catalog values; Fun Mode wobbles them like a real instrument, so do these in Ideal Mode.
