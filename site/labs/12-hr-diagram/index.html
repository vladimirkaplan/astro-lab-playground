Lab 12 · The H-R diagram

Two numbers per star: how hot, how bright. Plot those two against each other and stars refuse to scatter randomly - they line up, clump, and leave two empty corners. That arrangement is the most useful picture in astronomy, and every size you will read off it you will calculate yourself.

What this lab is about

A temperature is easy to get from a spectrum. A luminosity takes one more ingredient, usually a distance. Those two numbers look like very little information about a star. Plot them against each other for a few dozen stars, though, and the picture is not a cloud of dots: most stars fall on a single diagonal band, with a pocket of bright cool stars above it and a handful of faint hot stars stuck in the bottom left. Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell found that arrangement independently around 1911-1913, before anyone knew stars shine by fusing hydrogen, and it organized the whole subject.

How to read the axes

  • Temperature runs backwards. Hot stars are on the left. The axis is also compressed: 40,000 K to 2,500 K share one row of pixels, so the spacing between tick labels gets wider as you go right. Astronomers are stuck with this convention and so is this sim.
  • Luminosity is logarithmic. Each horizontal grid line is a factor of ten. The axes here span ten decades of power and the stars in this lab cover five of them, from Wolf 359 at 0.0014 times the Sun to Deneb at about 200,000. Read a vertical gap in decades, never in centimetres.
  • Luminosity is the star, not the view. It is the power the star actually emits, in solar units. The brightness of a star as it reaches a telescope depends on distance too, which is why the lab needs distances before it can build a diagram.

The machine behind the picture

A star is a hot sphere, so the power leaving it is set by its surface area and by the fourth power of its surface temperature. In solar units that is L = R² (T / 5772)⁴. Two of those three quantities are what you measure, so the third comes out for free:

R = sqrt(L) / (T / 5772)²

This is the whole trick of the diagram. It is why a cool star far above the main sequence is not a mystery but an enormous object, and why a hot star in the bottom left has to be tiny. Temperature and luminosity are the measurement; radius is the verdict.

Main sequence: where stars spend their lives

The diagonal band is the main sequence, and it is where stars fuse hydrogen. Position on it is set by mass, which is why the band is a sequence at all: more mass means a hotter core, a hotter surface, and a luminosity that climbs roughly as the 3.5 power of the mass. A 10 solar mass B star is a few thousand times more luminous than the Sun and burns its fuel in a few tens of millions of years; a 0.1 solar mass M dwarf like Proxima Centauri puts out 0.0017 times the Sun and will outlast the present age of the universe. The band has a thickness because stars of the same colour are not all exactly alike, and because real measurements carry error - switch to Fun Mode in the sim and watch the band fatten.

Giants and supergiants: same colour, far more of it

Take Arcturus and the Sun. Arcturus is cooler, yet it shines about a hundred times brighter. Cooler means less power per square metre of surface, so the only way to win that argument is to have far more surface: the sim puts Arcturus near 19 solar radii. Giants are stars that have run out of hydrogen in their cores and swollen. Supergiants go further - Betelgeuse comes out near 930 solar radii, about 4.3 AU, which is past the orbit of Mars. Rigel is the opposite combination and just as instructive: hot and 120,000 times the Sun, so roughly 79 solar radii, a blue supergiant.

White dwarfs: the bottom-left corner

Sirius B is 25,200 K, as hot as a B star, and puts out 0.026 times the Sun. Feed those into the radius formula and you get 0.0085 solar radii, about 5,900 km: the size of Earth, carrying the mass of a Sun. Nothing fuses down there; it is an exposed core cooling slowly. The reason the bottom-left corner of the diagram is nearly empty is that matter has no business being both very hot and very dim unless it is either tiny or heavily veiled.

What the diagram is not

It is not a map. Two stars drawn next to each other can be ten parsecs and ten thousand parsecs apart; the diagram plots properties, not positions, and a star drifting right on the screen means its surface cooled, not that it moved. Nor is the diagram a portrait of any single star forever: as a star ages it travels across it, which is why astronomers read cluster ages off the diagram by looking for the turnoff, the point where the most massive members have started leaving the main sequence.

Why this matters to astronomers: the H-R diagram turns a list of numbers into physics. It gives distances to star clusters and to other galaxies, it dates them, and it is the scoreboard that stellar evolution theory has to match. The lab drills the habit that makes it work: measure two things, derive the third, and know which is which. (Grounded in OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 21, CC BY 4.0.)

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Reading the horizontal axis left to right as hotter. It runs the other way: a star to the right of the Sun is cooler than the Sun.
  • Measuring a vertical gap with a ruler. The luminosity axis is logarithmic, so equal distances are equal ratios.
  • Concluding red means small and dim. Betelgeuse is one of the coolest stars on the plot and one of the biggest objects in it.
  • Explaining a white dwarf with fusion. It is hot because it is a fresh, dense remnant radiating stored heat, not because it burns anything.
  • Forgetting the fourth power. Double the temperature at fixed size and you get 16 times the luminosity, not twice.
  • Quoting the sim radius as if it were measured. Interferometers do measure a few dozen stellar diameters directly; everything here is derived from temperature and luminosity, which is what makes the number sensitive to bad data.
  • Treating the published luminosity class as gospel. Polaris is labelled class Ib, a supergiant, and still plots in the giant region of this diagram: labels are conventions, positions are measurements.

Reading log

Each dot is one real star, plotted from rounded public catalog values of effective temperature and luminosity (Yale Bright Star Catalog and standard star fact sheets; the more-cited value was used where sources disagree). Radii are never typed in: the sim computes them from those two numbers with the Stefan-Boltzmann law, so a radius here can sit 10-30 percent away from a directly measured one, and that gap is rounding plus real variability in the star. The three white dwarfs ship a luminosity derived from their published temperature and radius, because printed white-dwarf luminosities for the same star differ by a factor of two between sources. Dot colours are approximate blackbody swatches, and Fun Mode noise is a teaching illustration, not a photometric error model. This is a properties plot, not a map of the sky, and it holds no data about any student.

Read the two numbers off the sim, then derive the third yourself. Ideal Mode gives exact catalog values; Fun Mode wobbles them like a real instrument, so do these in Ideal Mode.
