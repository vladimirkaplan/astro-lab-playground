Lab 8 · Phases of Venus

One inner orbit, one angle. The shape in the telescope, the width of the disk, and the hour you are allowed to look all follow from where Venus sits between you and the Sun.

Words in this lab

  • phase — the lit shape of an object that you can see.
  • crescent — a thin sliver of lit shape.
  • gibbous — a lit shape that is more than half but not full.
  • elongation — how far a planet sits from the Sun in your sky, in degrees.
  • conjunction — when a planet lines up with the Sun in your sky. Inferior means it sits between us and the Sun; superior means it sits behind the Sun.
  • phase angle — the Sun-Venus-Earth angle. It sets the phase.
  • AU (astronomical unit) — the Earth-Sun distance, about 150 million km.
  • arcsecond — 1/3600 of a degree. A very small angle.

What this lab is about

Venus orbits inside Earth's orbit. That one fact controls everything a viewer sees. Venus never strays more than about 47 degrees from the Sun. It is never up at midnight. And it runs through a complete set of phases, like the Moon. It does one thing the Moon barely manages. It also changes size in the telescope, by a factor of about 6 across the cycle.

Where the phase comes from

Half of Venus is lit at all times. It is a ball in sunlight. The phase is how much of that lit half your seat on Earth can see. One angle sets it: the Sun-Venus-Earth angle, called the phase angle. Behind the Sun (superior conjunction) that angle is 0. The whole dayside faces you, so Venus looks full. Between Earth and Sun (inferior conjunction) it is 180. You are looking at the nightside, so Venus looks new. At greatest elongation it is exactly 90, and exactly half the disk is lit.

The lit side always points toward the Sun. That is a real test, not decoration. A crescent Venus drawn with its bright side away from the Sun is wrong, however neatly it is inked.

Phase and size move together

  • Inferior conjunction: 0.28 AU away, new, about 60 arcseconds across. The widest disk and the darkest one, buried in the Sun glare.
  • Greatest elongation, about 46 degrees from the Sun: 0.69 AU, half lit, about 24 arcseconds. The best night of the cycle to look.
  • Superior conjunction: 1.72 AU, full, about 10 arcseconds. A full Venus is the smallest Venus.

Same ball, three distances. A crescent Venus is about 6 times wider than a full one because it is about 6 times nearer. The two facts arrive together every cycle. If a drawing shows a fat crescent next to a fat full disk, the drawing contains two different planets.

Why it broke the Ptolemaic arrangement

In Ptolemy's model, Venus rides an epicycle. That is a small circle, and its centre stays locked to the Earth-Sun line. Venus is never carried around behind the Sun. So the model allows crescent and half phases, but it forbids gibbous and full. The same epicycle also holds Venus at roughly one distance. So the disk should hardly change size. Galileo turned a telescope on Venus in late 1610. He found the whole cycle, gibbous and nearly full disks included. And he saw a disk that swelled and shrank by roughly a factor of 6 in width. Turn the Ptolemaic prediction on in the sim and watch the second inset. It never passes half lit and never grows. One line of reasoning, one tube of lenses, one dead world system. (Galileo did not invent the telescope. He was the first to aim it at the sky and publish what he found.)

What to take from this

A single clean measurement settled a fight about the shape of the cosmos. The argument was geometric, not technical. Only a planet that orbits the Sun inside Earth's orbit can do this. It can show a full set of phases, with a matching change in apparent size. Learn the angle and the phase. Then the width and the hour to observe stop being three separate things to memorize.

Why this matters to astronomers: this is the cleanest case in the history of science. One observation rules out a rival model, outright. The skill is still in use. Sun-Earth-planet angles decide when a telescope can spend time on an inner planet at all. The same geometry schedules elongation and transit observing campaigns. Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Blaming Earth's shadow for the phases of Venus. Earth's shadow makes lunar eclipses. The day-night line of a phase is always a diameter of the disk.
  • Planning to find Venus at midnight. Venus stays within 47 degrees of the Sun, so it sets within a few hours of sunset. Or it rises a few hours before sunrise.
  • Drawing the greatest-elongation disk fully lit. Greatest elongation means phase angle 90, which means exactly half lit.
  • Assuming a bigger disk must be a fuller disk. The fattest disk in the eyepiece is the crescent, because a crescent is the near one.
  • Saying Galileo invented the telescope. He improved one and was the first to use it systematically on the sky.
  • Calling Venus new at superior conjunction. That one is full and tiny. The new one is the near one, hidden in the glare.
