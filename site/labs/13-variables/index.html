Lab 13 · Variable stars and Cepheids

Some stars breathe. Their brightness rises and falls on a clockwork schedule. That schedule is not decoration: it tells you the star's luminosity. Henrietta Leavitt found the rule in 1912, studying plate after plate of variable stars. Her rule became the rung of the distance ladder that let Hubble leave the Milky Way. In this lab you do the real move, twice. First you fold a light curve until it snaps into a period. Then you run that period through the period-luminosity line to get a distance.

Words in this lab

  • variable star — a star whose brightness changes with time.
  • Cepheid — a pulsing star whose brightening has a fixed period.
  • period — the time for one full cycle: one pulse, or one orbit.
  • luminosity — the total power a star gives off as light.
  • magnitude — a number for brightness. A smaller number means brighter.
  • light curve — a plot of brightness against time.
  • absolute magnitude — how bright a star would look from one set, far distance.
  • distance modulus — m minus M. A shortcut from magnitudes to distance.

What this lab is about

One slogan wrecks this lab: "Cepheids are standard candles." The slogan is wrong, and the wrongness matters. No single brightness fits all Cepheids. A Cepheid's luminosity depends on its period. Luminosity means the total power the star gives off as light. So the work is a two-step reduction. First you find the period. You pull it from a pile of scattered brightness readings. Then the period-luminosity relation turns that period into an absolute magnitude. Only then do you bring in the star's apparent brightness, which is how bright the star looks from Earth. That comparison gives you a distance. Skip step one and you have not measured anything. You have recited. Neither bench in this sim hands you the period of your star. You take it from the light curve yourself.

Reading a light curve (and its upside-down axis)

A light curve plots brightness against time. Astronomers draw it with the magnitude axis upside down. Smaller numbers, which mean brighter, sit at the top. So a light curve maximum is a valley in the plot. Learn that reflex before you read anything else here. The shapes you will fold say what kind of variable you have:

  • The sawtooth (Cepheids, RR Lyrae stars). Brightness rises fast, then falls slowly. In this lab's Cepheids the rise takes about a third of the period. For RR Lyrae stars it takes well under a tenth, so their fold looks like a hatchet. The asymmetry is real physics. The star's outer layers slam outward and brighten quickly. Then they relax back more slowly.
  • Flat with two dips (Algol). A square-bottomed, narrow eclipse on a flat baseline means something passes in front. Algol is two stars orbiting each other. Its "period" is an orbit, not a pulse. The dip depth depends on the viewing geometry, not on how luminous anything is. That is exactly why an eclipsing binary does not sit on the period-luminosity line. The same flat-plus-dip shape, at any depth, in any pair of stars: eclipses.
  • The slow, oversized roll (Mira). Mira swings five magnitudes over nearly a year. Dust and boiling gas reshape every apparition. Huge, beautiful, and useless as a yardstick.

Folding: the crank that finds the period

You cannot read the period from a raw light curve. The star is sampled at uneven times, and every reading carries noise. The points do not connect. So you fold. Pick a trial period. Wrap every observation onto a phase from 0 to 1. The phase is the fraction of the trial cycle. Now plot brightness against phase. A wrong trial period smears the fold into a blob. One phase then receives the star at several different brightnesses. If the period is right, the points snap onto one knife-thin curve. The sim scores how thin the fold is with the phase dispersion statistic (PDM, Stellingwerf's theta-squared). It compares the scatter inside the phase bins with the total scatter. Low means a sharp fold. The button in Bench 2 runs a proper period search on the same statistic. So you can check your hand-fold honestly.

Leavitt's move: why a period knows a luminosity

Leavitt's trick was to study variables in the Small Magellanic Cloud. Its stars are so far away that every one of them sits at essentially the same distance. Same distance means apparent magnitude order equals luminosity order, with no distances required. She found the brightest variables also had the longest periods: a tight relation between period and intrinsic brightness.

The relation is not a coincidence to memorise; it has a mechanism. A pulsation period is the whole star ringing like a bell. A bell's tone is set by its size and density, so the period tracks the star's mean density. Also, stars that pulsate at all live in a narrow vertical band of the H-R diagram. That band is the instability strip. Along that strip, luminosity tracks mass. A heavier strip star is more luminous and less dense. So it rings more slowly. Period and luminosity are two readings of the same hidden quantity. That is why one can calibrate the other. (Course framing follows OpenStax Astronomy 2e, "Cepheid Variables" and "Period-Luminosity Relation", CC BY 4.0; this page is original prose.)

The line the sim uses is M = -2.76 log(P) - 1.40 (V band, P in days). It is the standard classroom calibration for classical Cepheids from 1 to 100 days. Try it outside that window and the physics module refuses with an error. That is deliberate. A 0.45-day RR Lyrae star and a 332-day Mira are different families of pulsator. They belong off this line entirely.

Two yardsticks, and one star that is neither

RR Lyrae stars are old, low-mass pulsators with half-day periods. They all sit at nearly the same absolute magnitude, around +0.65: a single faint candle. They are dim, but there are millions of them in globular clusters and in the halo of our galaxy. Shapley used them to size the Milky Way. Classical Cepheids are ten to fifty times more luminous. They also bring their own ruler: each period has its own place on the line. That brighter reach is what let Hubble find Cepheids in Andromeda and leave the Galaxy behind. Mira is neither candle. It is a dusty, convection-boiling red giant. Its period drifts, and its light curve follows dust clouds, not a clean clock. The sim refuses to use Mira in the distance bench. Bench 1 lets you see why in one fold.

How this lab runs

The live dispersion readout tells you how close you are. Bench 2 asks you to "be Leavitt." A hidden Cepheid sits in an unseen star cluster. You get about 112 measurements over a few cycles, with no period given. You fold to find the period. You read the mean magnitude off the plot. Then you run the two-step reduction and press Check. The page grades your absolute magnitude, your distance modulus, and your cluster distance against the truth. It also logs the whole reduction. Ideal Mode is exact. Fun Mode adds a reproducible +-0.05 mag of seeded scatter, so the fold never comes out infinitely thin. That is what real data looks like. (Course framing follows OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 26, CC BY 4.0.)

Why this matters to astronomers: the Cepheid period-luminosity relation is a step of the distance ladder. Every modern value of the expansion rate of the universe leans on that step. The whole distance scale of the nearby universe is built by timing pulses and consulting one straight line. That is why measuring periods precisely, the thing you practice here, survived a century of telescopes. It still pays the bills.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Saying "Cepheids are standard candles" as if they all share one brightness. They do not. A Cepheid's absolute magnitude depends on its period. Extract the period first, or the candle has no calibration.
  • Running the period-luminosity line on anything with a light curve. It is the classical Cepheid line, valid from 1 to 100 days. An RR Lyrae star (0.45 d) or Mira (332 d) makes it throw an error. That error is the lesson.
  • Forgetting the inverted axis: maximum light is the smallest magnitude and sits at the TOP of the plot.
  • Reading the maximum magnitude where the mean belongs. The distance modulus needs the mean magnitude over the whole cycle. It does not want the brightest or the darkest point.
  • Modulus arithmetic: mu = m - M. With M negative, mu = 8.85 - (-4.16) = 13.01, not 4.69. Then d = 10^((mu+5)/5). Dropping the +5 shrinks every distance by a factor of 10.
  • Treating Algol's "period" as a pulse. The dips are eclipses - geometry, not luminosity. Eclipsing binaries do not sit on the period-luminosity line.
