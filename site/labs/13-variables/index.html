Lab 13 · Variable stars and Cepheids

Some stars breathe. Their brightness rises and falls on a clockwork schedule, and that schedule is not decoration - it is a measurement of the star's luminosity. Henrietta Leavitt found the rule in 1912 by studying hundreds of variable stars photographic plate by photographic plate, and it became the rung of the distance ladder that let Hubble leave the Milky Way. This lab makes you do the actual move, twice: fold a light curve until it snaps into a period, then run the period through the period-luminosity line to get a distance.

What this lab is about

There is a slogan that wrecks this lab: "Cepheids are standard candles." It is wrong in a way that matters. There is no single brightness a Cepheid has. A Cepheid's luminosity depends on its period, so the work is a two-step reduction: first you extract the period from a pile of scattered brightness measurements, then you convert that period into an absolute magnitude with the period-luminosity relation, and only then do you compare with the star's apparent brightness to get a distance. Skip step one and you have not measured anything - you have recited. Neither bench in this sim will hand you the period of the star you are supposed to reduce; you pull it out of the light curve yourself.

Reading a light curve (and its upside-down axis)

A light curve is brightness against time, drawn the way astronomers draw it: the magnitude axis is inverted, so smaller numbers (brighter) sit at the top and a light curve maximum is a valley in the plot. Learn that reflex before you read anything else here. The shapes you will fold say what kind of variable you have:

  • The sawtooth (Cepheids, RR Lyrae stars). Brightness rises fast, then declines slowly - for the Cepheids in this lab the rise takes about a third of the period, and for RR Lyrae stars well under a tenth of it, which is why the RR Lyrae fold looks like a hatchet. The asymmetry is real physics: the star's outer layers slam outward and brighten quickly, then relax back more slowly.
  • Flat with two dips (Algol). A square-bottomed, narrow eclipse on a flat baseline means something passes in front: Algol is two stars orbiting, and its "period" is an orbit, not a pulse. The dip depth depends on the viewing geometry, not on how luminous anything is - which is exactly why an eclipsing binary does not sit on the period-luminosity line. The same flat-plus-dip shape at any depth, in any pair of stars: eclipses.
  • The slow, oversized roll (Mira). A five-magnitude swing over nearly a year, with dust and convection reshaping every apparition. Huge, beautiful, and useless as a yardstick.

Folding: the crank that finds the period

You cannot read the period off a raw light curve that samples the star irregularly and noisy - the points don't connect. So you fold: pick a trial period, wrap every observation onto a phase from 0 to 1 (phase = fraction of the trial cycle), and plot brightness against phase. If the trial period is wrong, one phase receives the star at several different brightnesses and the fold smears into a blob. If it is right, the points snap onto one knife-thin curve. The sim scores how thin the fold is with the phase dispersion statistic (PDM, Stellingwerf's theta-squared): it measures the scatter of points inside phase bins against the total scatter, and low means a sharp fold. The button in Bench 2 runs a proper period search on the same statistic, so you can check your hand-fold honestly.

Leavitt's move: why a period knows a luminosity

Leavitt's trick was to study variables in the Small Magellanic Cloud, where - the stars being so far away - every one of them is at essentially the same distance. Same distance means apparent magnitude order equals luminosity order, no distances required. She found the brightest variables also had the longest periods: a tight relation between period and intrinsic brightness.

And the relation is not a coincidence to memorise; it has a mechanism. A pulsation period is the whole star ringing like a bell, and a bell's tone is set by size and density - the period tracks the star's mean density. Meanwhile the variables that pulsate at all live in a narrow vertical band of the H-R diagram, the instability strip, and along that strip luminosity tracks mass: more massive strip stars are more luminous and less dense, hence slower-ringing. Period and luminosity are two readings of the same underlying quantity, which is why one calibrates the other. (Course framing follows OpenStax Astronomy 2e, "Cepheid Variables" and "Period-Luminosity Relation", CC BY 4.0; this page is original prose.)

The line the sim uses, M = -2.76 log(P) - 1.40 (V band, P in days), is the standard classroom calibration for classical Cepheids over 1-100 days. Try it outside that window and the physics module refuses with an error - deliberately. A 0.45-day RR Lyrae star and a 332-day Mira are different families of pulsator and belong off this line entirely.

Two yardsticks, and one star that is neither

RR Lyrae stars - old, low-mass, half-a-day pulsators - all sit at nearly the same absolute magnitude (around +0.65): a single faint candle. Dim, but there are millions of them in globular clusters and the galactic halo, and Shapley used them to size the Milky Way. Classical Cepheids are ten to fifty times more luminous and bring their own ruler: each period has its own place on the line. That brighter reach is what let Hubble find Cepheids in Andromeda and leave the Galaxy behind. Mira is neither candle: a dusty, convection-boiling red giant whose period drifts and whose light curve depends on dust clouds, not on a clean clock - the sim refuses to use it in the distance bench, and Bench 1 lets you see why in one fold.

How this lab runs

Bench 1 is the light-curve bench: pick a catalog star, watch its raw scattered brightness in time, and drag the fold-period slider until the phase plot snaps into a knife-thin sawtooth; the live dispersion readout tells you how close you are. Bench 2 is "be Leavitt": a hidden Cepheid in an unseen star cluster, ~112 measurements over a few cycles, no period given. You fold to find the period, read the mean magnitude off the plot, run the two-step reduction, and press Check - the page grades your absolute magnitude, your distance modulus, and your cluster distance against the truth, and logs the whole reduction. Ideal Mode is exact; Fun Mode adds a reproducible +-0.05 mag of seeded photometric scatter, so the fold never comes out infinitely thin, which is what real data looks like. (Course framing follows OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Ch. 26, CC BY 4.0.)

Why this matters to astronomers: the Cepheid period-luminosity relation is the step of the distance ladder every modern value of the expansion rate of the universe leans on. The whole distance scale of the nearby universe is literally calibrated by timing pulses and consulting one straight line - which is why measuring periods precisely (the thing you are practicing here) survived a century of telescopes and still pays the bills.

Common mistakes in this lab:
  • Saying "Cepheids are standard candles" as if they all share one brightness. A Cepheid's absolute magnitude depends on its period - extract the period first or the candle has no calibration.
  • Running the period-luminosity line on anything with a light curve. It is the classical Cepheid line, valid 1-100 d: an RR Lyrae star (0.45 d) or Mira (332 d) makes it throw an error, and that error is the lesson.
  • Forgetting the inverted axis: maximum light is the smallest magnitude and sits at the TOP of the plot.
  • Reading the maximum magnitude where the mean belongs. The distance modulus needs the mean magnitude over the whole cycle, not the brightest or darkest point.
  • Modulus arithmetic: mu = m - M. With M negative, mu = 8.85 - (-4.16) = 13.01, not 4.69. Then d = 10^((mu+5)/5); dropping the +5 shrinks every distance by a factor of 10.
  • Treating Algol's "period" as a pulse. The dips are eclipses - geometry, not luminosity - and eclipsing binaries do not sit on the period-luminosity line.

Bench 2 - be Leavitt: the hidden cluster Cepheid

A variable star in an unseen star cluster, ~112 magnitude measurements over three and a half cycles. You get no period, no name, and no distance - just the plates. Do the reduction in order: (1) fold with the slider until the sawtooth snaps - that is the period; the "best period" button runs a proper search on the dispersion statistic so you can check your fold honestly. (2) Read the star's mean magnitude off the time plot: the middle of the light curve's band, not its top or bottom. (3) Press Check: your period goes through the classroom Cepheid line M = -2.76 log(P) - 1.40, your mean magnitude and that M give the distance modulus, and the modulus gives the cluster's distance - graded against the truth. The period-luminosity line M = -2.76 log(P) - 1.40 with an RR Lyrae candle at +0.65 is the standard classroom calibration in V over 1-100 d; modern calibrations differ at the level of a few tenths of a magnitude. 1 pc = 3.26156 ly follows NIST. Light-curve shapes are idealised sawtooth/eclipse models that put each star's extremes exactly on its catalog range.
