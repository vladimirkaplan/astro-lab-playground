Newton's gravitation and second-law ratios. A ratio-table trainer. It hands you two-column tables in the worksheet's shape — a relation on the left, a blank on the right — and the rows, forward and reversed, come from a fresh generator every attempt. Work the reverse rows hardest: they are the actual skill the handout tests. This is a rehearsal with made-up numbers, not a submission channel.

The one thing you need before you touch a ratio table

For a fixed pair of masses the product k = G m1 m2 is just a constant, so F = k / d2 and F = G m1 m2 / d2 give the same ratios: F1/F2 = (d2/d1)2 either way. The worksheet's notation question never changes a ratio answer.

Table A — the inverse-square law

Newton's law of gravitation says the force between two fixed masses goes as one over the square of the distance. Put two situations side by side and the masses cancel, leaving F1/F2 = (d2/d1)2. Forward rows give you the distance relation and ask for the force ratio; reverse rows give you the force relation (say 9 F1 = 25 F2) and ask for the distance relation — you square-root and remember which object is closer. The reverse rows are the point of the handout, so they are half of every table here.

Table B — the second law at constant force

Newton's second law is a = F/m. Hold the force fixed and let the mass vary, and acceleration runs backwards with mass: a1/a2 = m2/m1. Four times the mass gets a quarter of the acceleration. Same forward/reverse structure as Table A, just inverted ratios instead of squared ones.

Why the worked law only appears after you check

Each table shows its worked law line (F1/F2 = (d2/d1)2, or a1/a2 = m2/m1) only after you press Check. If you can write the law and reach for the right inverse (square it, or flip it) before the page tells you, you have the skill; the page exists to grade it and then explain the ones you missed.

How this page runs

Bench A and Bench B each render a fresh ratio table and grade the whole thing at once, within 2%. The live-ratio bench underneath has two mass sliders and one distance slider feeding a small readout: watch the F-ratio and a-ratio move as you drag, and watch them collapse to exactly 1 when you equalise the inputs. Every check appends a row to your attempt log, which you can export as CSV. All numbers are freshly generated, so this can never hand you your graded assignment's answers.

Common slips this trainer catches:
  • Forgetting the square: writing F1/F2 = d2/d1 instead of (d2/d1)2.
  • Not flipping on the inverse a = F/m rows: a1/a2 is m2/m1, not m1/m2.
  • In the reverse rows, reporting d2/d1 when the question asks for d1/d2.
  • Believing the k-versus-G m1 m2 notation changes the ratio. It cancels; it does not.
  • Letting the closer mass/object have the weaker value: an inverse relationship means the bigger side of one ratio is the smaller side of the other.
