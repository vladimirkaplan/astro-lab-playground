What the first homework set is really testing

Underneath the topic list, Homework 1 checks four habits: knowing what the big objects are, moving between units along a chain of exact factors, turning a travel time into a distance with a fixed speed, and reading a number written in scientific notation with an SI prefix on it. None of it is hard arithmetic. The marks get lost on careless chains, on mixing up prefixes, and on sign errors in exponents — all of which you can drill here without touching a graded number.

Stars, galaxies, and the rest

A star is a self-gravitating ball of hot gas whose core is dense and hot enough to fuse light nuclei into heavier ones; that fusion is why it shines. Our Solar System has exactly one — the Sun. Stars matter for three reasons worth memorising as a trio: they are the energy source (sunlight drives weather and photosynthesis), the element factories (fusion builds heavier nuclei and dying stars scatter them into space), and the gravity anchors (their mass sets what orbits them). A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of anything from a few million to a few trillion stars plus gas and dust — the Milky Way and Andromeda are examples. A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust inside a galaxy (a nursery or a graveyard of stars), and a supernova is the explosion that ends a massive star, briefly outshining its whole galaxy. Hubble sorted galaxies by shape into elliptical, spiral, and irregular. All of that is public astronomy, restated here in plain words.

The unit-factor chain

Every conversion in this course is one or more links of a short chain of exact equalities you are given: 1 km = 1000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 cm = 10 mm, 1 mi = 5280 ft, 1 ft = 12 in, 1 in = 2.54 cm exactly. To convert you multiply along the chain and cancel units as you go, so the answer carries the units you asked for. The Practice tab grades your number within 2% and then shows the exact chain your conversion implies — the ladder you are expected to write on paper. Whole-number links like mi→ft = 5280 should come out to the unit, not to two significant figures.

Light-travel distances, the course way

Distance = speed × time. The course hands out the rounded value c = 300,000 km/s and uses classroom time multipliers (60 s per minute, 60 min per hour, 24 h per day, 7 d per week, 365 d per year), so a light-week is 604,800 s × 300,000 km/s = 1.8144×1011 km. The honest caveat, printed on the bench: the precise vacuum value is 299,792.458 km/s, which changes these answers by only about 0.002%. Watch your significant figures — the inputs are 2-to-3-figure, so 2-to-3-figure answers are the honest ones.

Scientific notation and the prefix trap

Scientific notation writes a number as mantissa × 10exponent with the mantissa between 1 and 10. Prefixes are just hidden powers of ten: milli (m) = 10-3, micro (u or µ) = 10-6, nano (n) = 10-9, kilo (k) = 103, mega (M) = 106. So 500µm = 500 × 10-6 m = 5×10-4 m — mantissa 5, exponent −4. The trap the worksheet sets, and that this bench will catch you on: micro is a thousand times smaller than milli. Read a µ as an m and your exponent is wrong by exactly 3. Both mantissa and exponent are graded here.

Why this matters beyond the grade: chaining factors and normalising prefixes is the entire skill behind every later calculation in the course. A parallax, a light-year, a redshift, a telescope's light grasp — they are all "write the number properly, multiply along a chain, and cancel the units." Drill it here where the numbers reset every click.

Common slips this bench catches:
  • Reading µ (micro, 10-6) as m (milli, 10-3) — the exponent is then off by a factor of 1000.
  • Writing a mantissa outside [1, 10), e.g. 50 × 10-5 instead of 5 × 10-4.
  • Losing whole-number factors to rounding: 3 mi = 15,840 ft exactly, not 16,000.
  • Using the precise c when the question asks for the course's 300,000 km/s (or vice-versa) without checking which one the question wants.
  • Flipping geo/helio: geo = Earth-centred, helio = Sun-centred.
  • Calling a galaxy's star count the Solar System's star count. The Solar System has one star.
